## What you will learn

- What a **phrase** is and what a **clause** is
- What a **finite verb** is, and how to tell it from a **non-finite** verb
- The kinds of phrases: noun, verb, adjective, adverb and prepositional
- **Main clauses** and **subordinate clauses**: an introduction
- How to count the clauses in a sentence

## Why this matters

The difference between a phrase and a clause is the key to many topics:

- **Kinds of sentences:** a simple sentence has one clause; a compound or complex sentence has two or more.
- **Transformation of sentences:** we change a clause into a phrase (*because he was ill* → *because of his illness*), or a phrase into a clause.
- **Connectives:** *although* takes a clause; *despite* takes a phrase.
- **Non-finite verbs:** participle phrases and infinitive phrases are phrases, not clauses.

## The idea in simple words

Look at these two groups of words.

> *in the morning*
> *when the sun rose*

Both tell us **when**. But there is a difference.

*When the sun rose* has a subject (*the sun*) and a verb that shows time (*rose*). It is a **clause**.

*In the morning* has no subject and no verb. It is a **phrase**.

Now look at these:

> *We met **after the match**.* (phrase: no subject, no verb)
> *We met **after the match ended**.* (clause: subject *the match*, verb *ended*)

A clause is like a small sentence inside a sentence. A phrase is a group of words that works as one part of a sentence, but it is not a small sentence.

## Grammar words you need

**Finite verb:** a verb that shows **tense** (present or past) and changes with the **subject**. *She **writes**. They **write**. She **wrote**.* Every clause has one.

**Non-finite verb:** a verb form that does **not** show tense by itself and does not change with the subject. There are three kinds: the **infinitive** (*to write*), the ***-ing* form** (*writing*) and the **past participle** used without a helping verb (*written*).

**Phrase:** a group of words that works as one unit but has no subject + finite verb.

**Clause:** a group of words with a subject and a finite verb.

**Main clause (independent clause):** a clause that makes complete sense on its own. It can be a sentence by itself.

**Subordinate clause (dependent clause):** a clause that cannot stand alone. It depends on the main clause. It usually begins with a joining word like *because, when, if, that, who, which*.

## The rules

### Rule 1: A clause has a subject and a finite verb.

> *the bus **left***: subject *the bus*, finite verb *left* → clause
> *that she **is** honest*: subject *she*, finite verb *is* → clause
> *if it **rains***: subject *it*, finite verb *rains* → clause

### Rule 2: A phrase has no subject + finite verb.

> *in the morning* → phrase
> *a very old temple* → phrase
> *to win the match* → phrase (*to win* is non-finite)
> *running fast* → phrase (*running* is non-finite)

A phrase can be long. *The tall boy with the red bag near the gate* is one long phrase. It still has no finite verb.

### Rule 3: A finite verb shows tense and agrees with the subject. A non-finite verb does neither.

Try the **two tests** on a verb:

1. **Time test:** can you change it from present to past? *She **writes** → She **wrote***. Yes → finite.
2. **Subject test:** does it change when the subject changes? *She **writes** / They **write***. Yes → finite.

Now test a non-finite verb:

> *She wants **to write**.* / *She wanted **to write**.* / *They want **to write**.*

*To write* never changes. The tense is shown by *wants / wanted*. So *to write* is non-finite.

| Finite (shows tense) | Non-finite (no tense) |
|---|---|
| *He **plays**.* | *He likes **to play**.* (to-infinitive) |
| *He **played**.* | ***Playing** cricket is fun.* (*-ing* form) |
| *He **has played**.* | *the game **played** yesterday* (past participle alone) |

In a verb phrase like *has played* or *is playing*, the **first** auxiliary (*has, is*) is the finite part. The whole verb phrase counts as one finite verb.

### Rule 4: Name a phrase by its main word.

Every phrase has a main word (the **head**). The phrase is named after it.

| Kind of phrase | Main word | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Noun phrase | a noun or pronoun | ***a beautiful red saree***, ***the boys in the last bench*** |
| Verb phrase | a verb, with its auxiliaries | *She **has been waiting** for an hour.* |
| Adjective phrase | an adjective | *The coffee was **very hot**.* *She is **fond of music**.* |
| Adverb phrase | an adverb | *He ran **very quickly**.* *She spoke **quite softly**.* |
| Prepositional phrase | a preposition + a noun phrase | ***in the garden***, ***after lunch***, ***with great care*** |

### Rule 5: A phrase can do the job of a noun, an adjective or an adverb.

Older school grammars, and many Indian textbooks, name phrases by the **job** they do, not by their main word. You should know both ways.

| Job | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Noun phrase (as a noun) | *I like **to play chess**.* | object of *like* |
| Adjective phrase (as an adjective) | *She is a woman **of great courage**.* | describes *woman* (= *a courageous woman*) |
| Adverb phrase (as an adverb) | *He did the work **with great care**.* | tells how (= *carefully*) |

Notice: *of great courage* and *with great care* are **prepositional phrases** by their main word. By their job, one is an adjective phrase and one is an adverb phrase. If the exam asks for the job, give the job.

### Rule 6: A main clause can stand alone. A subordinate clause cannot.

> ***I stayed at home** because it was raining.*

- *I stayed at home* makes complete sense alone → **main clause**.
- *because it was raining* does not make sense alone. We ask, "Because it was raining... what?" → **subordinate clause**.

Subordinate clauses usually begin with a **subordinating word**:

| Kind | Starting words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun clause | that, what, whether, if, who, how, why | *I know **that he is honest**.* |
| Adjective (relative) clause | who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when | *The man **who came here** is my uncle.* |
| Adverb clause | when, because, if, although, so that, as, since, until | *We left **when the rain stopped**.* |

These are explained fully in [Kinds of sentences and clauses](/topics/t09-kinds-of-sentences/kinds-of-sentences/).

### Rule 7: Count the finite verbs to count the clauses.

Each clause has one finite verb. So:

**number of finite verbs = number of clauses**

> *Ravi **knows** the man who **lives** next door.*

Finite verbs: *knows, lives* → 2 clauses:

- Main clause: *Ravi knows the man*
- Subordinate clause: *who lives next door*

> *Hearing the noise, Kavya **ran** to the window to see what **had happened**.*

Finite verbs: *ran, had happened* → 2 clauses. (*Hearing* and *to see* are non-finite, so they do not make clauses.)

## Special cases

### 1. *-ing* and *to* groups are phrases, not clauses

> ***Walking to school**, I met Arun.* (phrase: *walking* is non-finite)
> *She came **to meet you**.* (phrase: *to meet* is non-finite)

Even though these groups seem to describe an action, they have no finite verb.

### 2. The joining word can be left out

In speech, *that* is often dropped from a noun clause or a relative clause.

> *I think **(that) she is right**.* (still a clause: *she is*)
> *The book **(that) I bought** is good.* (still a clause: *I bought*)

Count the finite verbs, not the joining words.

### 3. A command has an understood subject

> *Close the door.* (= *You close the door.*)

This is still a clause. The subject *you* is understood.

### 4. Shortened clauses after *than* and *as*

> *Priya is taller **than I** (am).*
> *He runs as fast **as his brother** (runs).*

The verb is left out but understood. Grammar books treat *than I (am)* as a clause.

### 5. *Before, after, since, until*: phrase or clause

> *We left **after the match**.* (phrase: a noun follows)
> *We left **after the match ended**.* (clause: subject + finite verb follow)

The joining word is the same. What follows decides.

### 6. One verb phrase, one clause

*Has been working*, *will have gone*, *should be done* each count as **one** finite verb, because they make one verb phrase.

> *She **has been working** here for ten years.* → 1 clause

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Walking in the rain* → "clause" | **phrase** | *Walking* is non-finite. There is no subject + finite verb. |
| *He wants to go* → "two clauses" | **one clause** | *To go* is non-finite. Only *wants* is finite. |
| *She has been sleeping* → "three finite verbs" | **one** finite verb phrase | *Has been sleeping* is one verb phrase in one clause. |
| *Because it was late.* (written as a full sentence) | *We went home **because it was late**.* | A subordinate clause cannot stand alone. It needs a main clause. |
| *I know he is right* → "one clause" | **two clauses** | *That* is left out, but *he is right* still has a subject and a finite verb. |
| *a man of courage*: *of courage* → "adverb phrase" | **adjective phrase** | It describes the noun *man*. A phrase that describes a noun does an adjective's job. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Phrase or clause?** Say whether the underlined part is a phrase or a clause.
- **Pick out the finite verbs** and say how many clauses there are.
- **Name the kind of phrase** (noun, adjective, adverb...).
- **Mark the main clause and the subordinate clause.**
- **Replace a clause with a phrase** (or the reverse). This leads into [transformation of sentences](/topics/t09-kinds-of-sentences/kinds-of-sentences/).

Follow these steps:

1. **Find every verb** in the sentence.
2. **Test each verb:** does it show tense and change with the subject? Keep only the finite ones.
3. **Each finite verb = one clause.** Find its subject.
4. **Find the main clause:** the one that makes sense alone.
5. **The others are subordinate.** Look for their joining words.
6. **Anything without a finite verb is a phrase.** Name it by its main word or its job, as the question asks.

### Worked example 1

> *Phrase or clause? She came home **before sunset**.*

1. Verbs in the bold part: none.
2. *Before sunset* = preposition + noun. No subject, no finite verb.

**Answer:** **phrase** (a prepositional phrase that does the job of an adverb: it tells when).

### Worked example 2

> *Count the clauses: The boy who won the prize is my cousin.*

1. Verbs: *won, is*.
2. Both are finite: *won* (past), *is* (present, agrees with *the boy*).
3. Two finite verbs → **two clauses**.
4. Main clause: *The boy is my cousin* (makes sense alone).
5. Subordinate clause: *who won the prize* (begins with *who*, cannot stand alone).

**Answer:** **two clauses**: main *The boy ... is my cousin*; subordinate *who won the prize*.

### Worked example 3

> *Pick out the finite verbs: Having finished his homework, Karthik went out to play.*

1. Verbs: *having finished, went, to play*.
2. *Having finished*: no tense change with subject → non-finite. *Went*: past tense → finite. *To play*: non-finite.
3. One finite verb → one clause.

**Answer:** The only finite verb is ***went***. The sentence has **one clause** (it is a simple sentence).

### Worked example 4

> *Replace the bold clause with a phrase: The girl **who has long hair** is my sister.*

1. The clause describes *the girl*: it does an adjective's job.
2. A phrase with the same meaning: *with long hair*.

**Answer:** *The girl **with long hair** is my sister.*

## Quick check

1. Phrase or clause? *She smiled **when she saw her mother**.*
2. Phrase or clause? *He sat **under the banyan tree**.*
3. How many clauses? *I hope that you will come to Madurai.*
4. Name the phrase by its job: *He answered **in a polite manner**.*
5. Is *to finish* finite or non-finite? *We need to finish this today.*

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **Clause.** It has a subject (*she*) and a finite verb (*saw*).
2. **Phrase.** *Under the banyan tree* has no subject and no verb. It is a prepositional phrase.
3. **Two.** Finite verbs: *hope* and *will come*. Main clause: *I hope*. Subordinate (noun) clause: *that you will come to Madurai*.
4. **Adverb phrase.** It tells **how** he answered (= *politely*).
5. **Non-finite.** *To finish* is an infinitive. It does not show tense or change with the subject. The finite verb is *need*.

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **Clause** = subject + finite verb. **Phrase** = no subject + finite verb.
- **Finite verb:** shows tense and changes with the subject. ***to*-infinitive, *-ing* form, V3 alone** are non-finite.
- **Number of finite verbs = number of clauses.**
- **Main clause** stands alone. **Subordinate clause** depends on it and usually starts with *that, who, which, when, because, if...*
- Phrases are named by their main word (noun, verb, adjective, adverb, prepositional) or by their job (noun, adjective, adverb).
:::
