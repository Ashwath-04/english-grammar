- the formula of all 12 tenses (*has / have* + V3, *was / were* + V4, *had* + V3...)
- that the present perfect is connected to now, and the past simple goes with a finished past time
- that the past perfect shows the earlier of two past actions
- *since* + a point of time, *for* + a period of time
- that stative verbs (*know, believe, own*) take no continuous form

## What you will learn

- How to choose between two tenses that look similar:
  - present perfect or past simple
  - past simple or past perfect
  - perfect or perfect continuous
- How a story moves between the past simple, past continuous and past perfect
- The **sequence of tenses** in complex sentences, and its exceptions
- How to use *for, since, already, yet, just, ever* and *never*
- The errors examiners look for: *~~I am knowing~~*, *~~since two years~~*, *~~did not went~~* and more

## Why this matters

In Class 11 you learnt how each tense is built. In Class 12, the questions are harder: you must **choose** the right tense from the meaning of the whole sentence or paragraph.

Cloze passages, error correction and "fill in with the correct tense" all test this skill. The same skills are needed in [Reported speech](/topics/t12-reported-speech/class-12/), where tenses change, and in essay and letter writing.

## The idea in simple words

Look at these two sentences:

> *Kavya **has lost** her phone.*
> *Kavya **lost** her phone last week.*

Both are about the past. The first one makes you think about **now**: she does not have her phone now. The second one is a story about **last week**. Maybe she has found it again.

The tense does not only show time. It shows **what the speaker is thinking about**. Choosing a tense means asking: *Am I talking about now, or about a time that is over? Which action came first? Am I interested in the result, or in how long it took?*

## Grammar words you need

**Context:** the words and sentences around a sentence, which help us decide its meaning and tense.

**Narrative:** a story, or an account of events in the past.

**Main clause:** the part of a sentence that makes sense alone. *He said **that he was tired***: main clause *He said*. See [Phrase and clause](/foundation/f5-phrase-clause/).

**Subordinate clause:** a clause that depends on the main clause. In *He said that he was tired*, the subordinate clause is *that he was tired*.

**Sequence of tenses:** the rule that the tense in the subordinate clause usually follows the tense in the main clause.

**Universal truth:** a fact that is always true: *The earth goes round the sun.*

## The rules

### Rule 1: Use the past simple when the time is finished or stated. Use the present perfect when it is connected to now and no finished time is given.

| Past simple | Present perfect |
|---|---|
| a finished time is given or understood | no finished time; we think about now |
| *I **saw** that film **last week**.* | *I **have seen** that film.* (I know the story now.) |
| *Arun **went** to Delhi **in 2022**.* | *Arun **has been** to Delhi.* (his experience) |
| *She **lived** in Salem **for ten years**.* (She doesn't live there now.) | *She **has lived** in Salem **for ten years**.* (She still lives there.) |
| ***When** did you **arrive**?* | *How long **have** you **been** here?* |

Words that need the **past simple**: *yesterday, last week, ago, in 2019, when I was a child, then, at that time, When...?*

Words that need the **present perfect**: *just, already, yet, ever, never, so far, up to now, recently, since...*

> *I **have finished** my homework.* ✔
> *I **finished** my homework an hour ago.* ✔
> *~~I have finished my homework an hour ago.~~* ✘ (*ago* is a finished time)

**American English note:** Americans often use the past simple with *just, already* and *yet*: *Did you eat yet?* British English and Indian school English use the present perfect: ***Have** you **eaten** yet?* Use the British form in exams.

### Rule 2: Use the past perfect when you need to show that one past action happened before another.

> *When I reached the bus stop, the bus **left**.*
> (I reached first. Then the bus left. Maybe I got on it.)
>
> *When I reached the bus stop, the bus **had left**.*
> (The bus left first. I missed it.)

The past perfect changes the meaning. Use it when the order matters.

If you tell the actions **in the order they happened**, the past simple is enough:

> *The bus **left**, and then I **reached** the stop.* (no past perfect needed)

Common patterns with the past perfect:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| *By the time* + past simple, past perfect | *By the time the police came, the thief **had escaped**.* |
| past simple + *because* + past perfect | *She was tired because she **had walked** ten kilometres.* |
| *after* + past perfect, past simple | *After he **had finished** his meal, he went out.* |
| *never / ever... before* | *I **had** never **seen** snow before I went to Shimla.* |
| *No sooner... than / Hardly... when* | *No sooner **had** the bell **rung** than the children ran out.* |

With *after* and *before*, the order is already clear, so the past simple is also accepted: *After he finished his meal, he went out.*

### Rule 3: Use the perfect for a completed result or "how many". Use the perfect continuous for "how long" or an activity that may still be going on.

| Present perfect (result, number) | Present perfect continuous (duration, activity) |
|---|---|
| *I **have read** three chapters.* (how many: finished) | *I **have been reading** for three hours.* (how long) |
| *She **has painted** the room.* (finished: the room is ready) | *She **has been painting** the room.* (she may not be finished; there is paint on her hands) |
| *He **has written** five letters today.* | *He **has been writing** letters all day.* |

**Key test:** if the sentence gives a **number** of things done (*three chapters, five letters*), use the perfect, not the perfect continuous. *~~I have been reading three chapters~~* is wrong.

The same difference exists in the past:

> *By noon, she **had answered** all the questions.* (finished)
> *She **had been answering** questions for two hours when the bell rang.* (how long)

With stative verbs, use the perfect, even for "how long": *I **have known** him for years* (not *~~have been knowing~~*).

With some verbs (*live, work, teach, study, wait*), both forms are correct and mean almost the same: *I **have lived** / **have been living** here for five years.*

### Rule 4: In a story, the past simple moves the story forward, the past continuous gives the background, and the past perfect goes back in time.

> *It **was raining** heavily. People **were hurrying** home. Ravi **stood** at the bus stop. He **had missed** the last bus. Suddenly a car **stopped** beside him. It **was** his old teacher.*

| Tense | Job in the story | Examples from above |
|---|---|---|
| Past continuous | the background scene, actions already going on | *was raining, were hurrying* |
| Past simple | the main events, one after another | *stood, stopped, was* |
| Past perfect | an event that happened before the story's "now" | *had missed* |

When you fill in a cloze passage, first find the **main time** of the story. Then decide whether each verb is a main event, the background, or something that happened earlier.

### Rule 5: When the main verb is in the past, the verb in the subordinate clause is usually in the past too.

This is the **sequence of tenses**.

> *He **says** that he **is** tired.* (present → any tense)
> *He **said** that he **was** tired.* (past → past)
> *I **knew** that she **would** come.* (not *~~will come~~*)
> *She **told** me that she **had finished** the work.*
> *We **hoped** that it **would** not rain.*

| Main clause in the past | Subordinate clause |
|---|---|
| present simple idea | past simple: *He said he **was** busy.* |
| present continuous idea | past continuous: *She said she **was waiting**.* |
| present perfect / past idea | past perfect: *He said he **had seen** it.* |
| *will / can / may* | *would / could / might*: *I thought you **would** help.* |

If the main verb is in the **present** or **future**, the subordinate clause can be in any tense the meaning needs:

> *She **says** that she **was** ill yesterday and **will** come tomorrow.*

This rule is also the base of [Reported speech](/topics/t12-reported-speech/class-11/).

### Rule 6: The sequence-of-tenses rule has three main exceptions.

**(a) Universal truths and scientific facts** stay in the present simple.

> *The teacher said that the earth **goes** round the sun.*
> *We learnt that water **boils** at 100 °C.*

**(b) Habits or facts that are still true** may stay in the present.

> *She told me that she **goes** for a walk every morning.* (she still does)

(*went* is also correct here. The present shows that it is still true.)

**(c) Comparisons with *than* or *as*** take whatever tense the meaning needs.

> *He **liked** you more than he **likes** me.*
> *She **was** as kind then as she **is** now.*

### Rule 7: Use *since* with a point of time and *for* with a period. *Since* clauses take the past simple.

| *since* + a point of time | *for* + a period of time |
|---|---|
| *since Monday* | *for three days* |
| *since 2019* | *for five years* |
| *since 8 o'clock* | *for two hours* |
| *since morning* | *for a long time* |
| *since my childhood* | *for ages* |

> *I have been waiting **for** two hours.* / *I have been waiting **since** six o'clock.*

The main clause usually has a **perfect** tense. The clause after *since* uses the **past simple**:

> *I **have known** Meena **since** we **were** in Class 1.*
> *It **has rained** every day **since** we **came** to Kodaikanal.*

*For* can be used with any tense: *I lived in Delhi **for** two years* (past simple, finished). *Since* needs a perfect tense in the main clause.

*It is* + period + *since* + past simple:

> ***It is** five years **since** I last **saw** him.* (= *I have not seen him for five years.*)

### Rule 8: Use *already*, *yet*, *just*, *ever* and *never* with the present perfect, each in its own place.

| Word | Meaning | Used in | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *just* | a very short time ago | positive | between *have* and V3 | *The train **has just** arrived.* |
| *already* | sooner than expected | positive (and questions showing surprise) | between *have* and V3, or at the end | *I **have already** paid the fees.* |
| *yet* | up to now (expected to happen) | negative and questions | at the end | *Has she replied **yet**?* / *She hasn't replied **yet**.* |
| *ever* | at any time in your life | questions | between subject and V3 | *Have you **ever** been to Rameswaram?* |
| *never* | at no time | positive verb with negative meaning | between *have* and V3 | *I **have never** eaten durian.* |

*Already* in a question shows surprise:

> *Have you **already** finished? That was quick!*

*Never* is already negative. Do not add *not*: *~~I have not never seen~~* ✘.

## Special cases

### 1. *When...?* questions take the past simple

*When* asks for a finished time, so it cannot go with the present perfect.

> ***When did** you **come**?* (not *~~When have you come?~~*)

### 2. *This is the first time* + present perfect

> *This is the first time I **have eaten** a Kerala sadya.* (not *~~I eat~~* or *~~I am eating~~*)
> *It was the first time she **had flown** in a plane.* (past → past perfect)

### 3. Present perfect for the future after time words

After *when, after, as soon as, until*, use the present perfect (not the future perfect) to show that one future action will be complete before another.

> *I will go out **after** I **have finished** my homework.*
> *You can leave **as soon as** you **have signed** the register.*

### 4. *Since* with *ago* is wrong

> *~~I have lived here since two years ago.~~* ✘
> *I have lived here **for** two years.* ✔
> *I came here two years **ago**.* ✔

*Ago* goes with the past simple. *Since* needs a point, not a period.

### 5. *Recently* and *lately*

Both mean "in the near past, up to now", and usually take the present perfect.

> *Have you seen any good films **lately**?*
> *Prices **have gone** up **recently**.*

*Recently* can also take the past simple when it means "a short time ago": *I **recently met** your father.*

### 6. Present continuous used wrongly for "how long"

In some Indian English, the present continuous is used with *since* or *for*. This is not standard.

> *~~I am working here since 2020.~~* ✘
> *I **have been working** here since 2020.* ✔

### 7. Present simple for summaries and headlines

Newspaper headlines and story summaries often use the present simple for past events: *India **wins** the cup.* *In the story, the boy **finds** a treasure.* This is correct in those styles.

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I ~~am knowing~~ the answer.* | *I **know** the answer.* | *Know* is stative. No continuous form. |
| *She has been ill ~~since two years~~.* | *She has been ill **for two years**.* | *Two years* is a period. Use *for*. |
| *He ~~did not went~~ to the meeting.* | *He **did not go** to the meeting.* | After *did*, use V1. |
| *I ~~have seen~~ him two days ago.* | *I **saw** him two days ago.* | *Ago* is a finished time. Use the past simple. |
| *~~When have you come~~ back?* | ***When did** you **come** back?* | *When* asks for a finished time → past simple. |
| *He said that he ~~will~~ come.* | *He said that he **would** come.* | Sequence of tenses: past main verb → *would*. |
| *The teacher said that the sun ~~rose~~ in the east.* | *The teacher said that the sun **rises** in the east.* | A universal truth stays in the present. |
| *I ~~am living~~ here since 2018.* | *I **have been living** here since 2018.* | From a past point up to now → present perfect continuous. |
| *I ~~have been reading~~ three books this month.* | *I **have read** three books this month.* | A number of completed things → present perfect, not continuous. |
| *He ~~has came~~ late.* | *He **has come** late.* | After *has*, use V3. *Come – came – come*. |
| *I have ~~not never~~ seen it.* | *I have **never** seen it.* | *Never* is already negative. Two negatives are wrong. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Fill in with the correct tense form**, where two tenses look possible.
- **Identify the tense** in a passage.
- **Spot and correct the error**, often a sequence-of-tenses or *since / for* error.
- **Cloze passage** in the past: a story with blanks.

### Steps for choosing the tense in context

1. **Read the whole sentence or passage first.** Find the main time of the story.
2. **Look for signal words:** a finished time (*ago, last, yesterday, When?*) → past simple. *Just, already, yet, ever, since* → present perfect.
3. **Two past actions?** Which came first? The earlier one may need the past perfect.
4. **Result or number** → perfect. **How long / activity** → perfect continuous.
5. **Is the main verb in the past?** Then the subordinate verb usually goes into the past too, unless it is a universal truth.
6. **Check stative verbs, *if / when* clauses, and the V1 after *did*.**

### Worked example 1

> *I ______ (not see) Priya since she ______ (move) to Coimbatore.*

1. Signal word: *since* → the main clause needs a perfect tense.
2. Main clause: up to now → present perfect. *I* → *have*. Negative → *have not seen*.
3. *Since* clause: the past point → past simple → *moved*.

**Answer:** *I **have not seen** Priya since she **moved** to Coimbatore.*

### Worked example 2

> *Correct the error: The doctor told me that I will be fine soon.*

1. Main verb: *told* (past).
2. Sequence of tenses: past main verb → *will* becomes *would*.
3. Is it a universal truth? No.

**Answer:** *The doctor told me that I **would** be fine soon.*

### Worked example 3

> *Fill in: I'm tired. I ______ (study) since morning, but I ______ (finish) only two chapters.*

1. First blank: *since morning*, and the activity explains why I'm tired → how long → present perfect continuous → *have been studying*.
2. Second blank: a number of completed things (*two chapters*) → present perfect → *have finished*.

**Answer:** *I'm tired. I **have been studying** since morning, but I **have finished** only two chapters.*

### Worked example 4

> *Correct the error: When we arrived at the hall, the function already started.*

1. Two past actions: *arrived* and *started*.
2. Which came first? The function started before we arrived (*already*).
3. The earlier action → past perfect → *had already started*.

**Answer:** *When we arrived at the hall, the function **had already started**.*

### Worked example 5 (cloze)

> *Last Deepavali, our family ______ (1. go) to my grandmother's village. When we ______ (2. reach), she ______ (3. make) sweets in the kitchen. She ______ (4. prepare) them since the morning. We ______ (5. never taste) such good athirasam before.*

1. ***went***: a main event in the past story → past simple.
2. ***reached***: a short main event → past simple.
3. ***was making***: going on when we reached → past continuous.
4. ***had been preparing***: went on for a period (*since the morning*) up to a past point → past perfect continuous.
5. ***had never tasted***: experience up to that past point (*before*) → past perfect.

## Quick check

1. *I ______ (lose) my wallet. Can you lend me fifty rupees?*
2. *Shakespeare ______ (write) many plays.*
3. *She ______ (wait) for an hour when the bus finally came.*
4. Correct the error: *He told me that he is going to Madurai.*
5. *Have you finished your project ______?* (already / yet / just)

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. ***have lost.*** The result matters now (I have no money now) → present perfect.
2. ***wrote.*** Shakespeare is dead, so his life is a finished time → past simple. (*has written* would suggest he could still write more.)
3. ***had been waiting.*** A period (*for an hour*) before a past action (*came*) → past perfect continuous.
4. ***He told me that he was going to Madurai.*** Past main verb (*told*) → the subordinate verb moves into the past.
5. ***yet.*** *Yet* is used in questions and negatives, at the end. (*Already* is possible if you are surprised, but it would come before *finished*.)

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **Finished time** (*ago, last, yesterday, When?*) → past simple. **Connected to now** (*just, already, yet, ever, since*) → present perfect.
- **Earlier of two past actions** → past perfect, when the order matters.
- **How many / result** → perfect. **How long / activity** → perfect continuous.
- Stories: past continuous = background; past simple = events; past perfect = earlier events.
- **Past main verb → past subordinate verb.** Exceptions: universal truths, facts still true, comparisons.
- *since* + point, *for* + period. *Since* clause → past simple. Never *since two years*.
:::
