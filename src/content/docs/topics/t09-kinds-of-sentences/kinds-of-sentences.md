This page covers both classes. **Part 1** (kinds of sentences and clauses) is in the **Class 12** textbook. **Part 2** (transformation of sentences) is in the **Class 11** textbook. Part 1 comes first because you need to know clauses before you can transform sentences.

## What you will learn

**Part 1: Kinds of sentences and clauses (Class 12)**

- Sentences by **function**: assertive, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory (and optative)
- Sentences by **structure**: simple, compound and complex
- How to find **finite verbs** and count the clauses
- **Main** (independent) and **subordinate** (dependent) clauses
- The three kinds of subordinate clause: **noun**, **adjective (relative)** and **adverb** clauses, and the kinds of adverb clauses

**Part 2: Transformation of sentences (Class 11)**

- Changing **simple ↔ compound ↔ complex**
- The tools: participles and infinitives (simple), coordinating conjunctions (compound), subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns (complex)
- **Combining** several short sentences into one, and **splitting** a long one
- Other transformations: **affirmative ↔ negative**, **assertive ↔ interrogative**, **exclamatory ↔ assertive**, and **removing *too*** (*too ... to* ↔ *so ... that ... not*)

## Why this matters

"Do as directed" questions in both Class 11 and Class 12 depend on this lesson: *Change into a complex sentence. Combine into a simple sentence. Identify the clause.*

Good writing also needs variety. A paragraph of only simple sentences sounds childish. A paragraph of only long complex sentences is hard to read. Knowing how to change one kind into another lets you write clearly and well.

## The idea in simple words

Here is the same idea written in three ways.

> *Being ill, Ravi stayed at home.*
> *Ravi was ill, **so** he stayed at home.*
> ***As** Ravi was ill, he stayed at home.*

All three mean the same. But they are built differently.

- The first has **one** finite verb (*stayed*). *Being* is not finite. This is a **simple** sentence.
- The second has **two** finite verbs (*was, stayed*), joined by *so*. Both parts can stand alone. This is a **compound** sentence.
- The third also has **two** finite verbs, but one part (*As Ravi was ill*) cannot stand alone. It depends on the other. This is a **complex** sentence.

Changing a sentence from one kind to another, without changing its meaning, is called **transformation**.

## Grammar words you need

**Finite verb:** a verb that shows tense and agrees with the subject: *stayed, is, has gone, will come*. Every clause has exactly one. See [Phrase and clause](/foundation/f5-phrase-clause/).

**Non-finite verb:** an infinitive (*to go*), gerund or participle (*going, gone, having gone*). It does not make a clause. See [Non-finite verbs](/topics/t07-non-finite-verbs/class-12/).

**Clause:** a group of words with a subject and a finite verb.

**Main clause (independent clause):** a clause that makes complete sense alone.

**Subordinate clause (dependent clause):** a clause that cannot stand alone. It begins with a subordinating word (*because, when, that, who, if...*).

**Coordinating conjunction:** joins two equal clauses: *and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*.

**Subordinating conjunction:** joins a subordinate clause to a main clause: *because, although, when, if, unless, that, so that...*

**Relative pronoun:** *who, whom, whose, which, that*. It begins an adjective clause and refers to a noun before it.

**Transformation:** changing the form of a sentence without changing its meaning.

**Synthesis:** combining two or more sentences into one.

## The rules, part 1: kinds of sentences and clauses (Class 12)

### Rule 1: By function, a sentence states, asks, commands, exclaims or wishes.

| Kind | What it does | Ends with | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Assertive (declarative)** | states a fact or opinion (positive or negative) | . | *Chennai is a coastal city.* / *I don't like tea.* |
| **Interrogative** | asks a question | ? | *Where do you live?* |
| **Imperative** | gives a command, request or advice | . or ! | *Close the door.* / *Please sit down.* |
| **Exclamatory** | shows strong feeling | ! | *What a beautiful sunset!* / *How tall he is!* |
| **Optative** | expresses a wish or prayer | ! or . | *May God bless you!* / *Long live the King!* |

Many grammar books include the optative under assertive or exclamatory. The TN textbooks usually list the first four.

### Rule 2: By structure, a sentence is simple, compound or complex.

| Kind | Clauses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | one independent clause (one finite verb) | *The boy **ran** home.* |
| **Compound** | two or more independent clauses, joined by a coordinating conjunction (or a semicolon) | *The bell **rang**, **and** the boy **ran** home.* |
| **Complex** | one main clause + one or more subordinate clauses | ***When** the bell **rang**, the boy **ran** home.* |

A sentence with two or more independent clauses **and** at least one subordinate clause is called **compound-complex**:

> *I called Meena, but she did not answer **because she was asleep**.*

### Rule 3: Count the finite verbs to count the clauses.

**Number of finite verbs = number of clauses.**

> *Arun **likes** to play cricket in the evening.* → 1 finite verb (*likes*) → 1 clause → **simple**
> *Arun **plays** cricket, but his sister **plays** chess.* → 2 → **compound** (joined by *but*)
> *Arun, who **lives** next door, **plays** cricket.* → 2 → **complex** (joined by *who*)

*To play*, *playing*, *played* (with no helping verb), *having played* are non-finite. Do not count them.

A verb phrase (*has been playing, will be going*) counts as **one** finite verb.

### Rule 4: A main clause can stand alone. A subordinate clause depends on it.

> ***I will call you** when I reach Chennai.*

- *I will call you*: makes sense alone → **main clause**.
- *when I reach Chennai*: "When I reach Chennai... what?" → **subordinate clause**.

In a **compound** sentence, every clause is a main clause (they are equal):

> ***She sang** and **he danced**.* (two main clauses)

To find the subordinate clause, look for the joining word: *that, who, which, whose, when, where, because, if, although, so that...* The subordinate clause starts with it.

### Rule 5: A noun clause does the job of a noun.

A noun clause can be the subject, the object, the complement, or the object of a preposition.

| Job | Example |
|---|---|
| Subject | ***What he said** is true.* |
| Object of a verb | *I know **that he is honest**.* |
| Complement (after *be*) | *The truth is **that he lied**.* |
| Object of a preposition | *Listen to **what the teacher says**.* |
| In apposition (explains a noun) | *The news **that India won** made us happy.* |

**Starting words:** *that, whether, if, what, who, whom, whose, which, when, where, why, how*.

**Test:** replace the clause with *it* or *this* (or *something*). If the sentence still works, it is a noun clause.

> *I know **that he is honest**.* → *I know **it**.* ✔ → noun clause

*That* is often left out of a noun clause: *I think **(that) she is right**.*

### Rule 6: An adjective clause (relative clause) describes a noun or pronoun.

It comes **right after** the noun it describes.

> *The man **who came here** is my uncle.* (which man?)
> *This is the book **which I bought yesterday**.* (which book?)
> *The girl **whose bag was stolen** is crying.*
> *I remember the day **when we first met**.*
> *This is the house **where Bharathiyar lived**.*

| Starting word | Used for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *who* | people (subject) | *the boy **who** won* |
| *whom* | people (object) | *the boy **whom** we met* |
| *whose* | possession | *the girl **whose** father is a doctor* |
| *which* | things, animals | *the bus **which** goes to Salem* |
| *that* | people or things (defining only) | *the book **that** I lost* |
| *where* | place | *the town **where** I was born* |
| *when* | time | *the year **when** we moved* |
| *why* | reason (after *the reason*) | *the reason **why** he left* |

**Defining and non-defining clauses:**

> *My brother **who lives in Delhi** is a doctor.* (defining: I have more than one brother; this one lives in Delhi)
> *My brother, **who lives in Delhi**, is a doctor.* (non-defining, with commas: extra information about my only brother)

A non-defining clause has commas and never uses *that*: *~~My brother, that lives in Delhi, ...~~* ✘

**Test:** the clause comes after a noun and answers "which one?" or "what kind?".

### Rule 7: An adverb clause does the job of an adverb. It tells when, where, why, how, with what result, or on what condition.

| Kind | Question it answers | Starting words | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Time** | when? | when, while, as, before, after, as soon as, until, till, since, whenever | *Wait here **until I come back**.* |
| **Place** | where? | where, wherever | *Sit **wherever you like**.* |
| **Reason (cause)** | why? | because, since, as | *He was absent **because he was ill**.* |
| **Purpose** | for what purpose? | so that, in order that, lest | *She works hard **so that she may succeed**.* |
| **Result** | with what result? | so ... that, such ... that | *He was **so** tired **that he fell asleep**.* |
| **Condition** | on what condition? | if, unless, provided (that), as long as, in case | ***If it rains**, we will stay home.* |
| **Contrast (concession)** | in spite of what? | although, though, even though, even if, while, whereas | ***Although he is rich**, he is not happy.* |
| **Comparison** | compared with what? | than, as ... as | *She is taller **than her brother is**.* |
| **Manner** | how? | as, as if, as though | *Do it **as I told you**.* / *He talks **as if he knows everything**.* |

*Lest* means "so that ... not" and is followed by *should*: *Walk carefully **lest you should fall**.* (formal)

### Rule 8: Decide the kind of clause by its job, not by its first word.

The same word can start different kinds of clauses.

| Sentence | Clause | Job | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| *I know **where he lives**.* | where he lives | object of *know* (= *I know it*) | noun |
| *This is the house **where he lives**.* | where he lives | describes *the house* | adjective |
| *Sit **where you like**.* | where you like | tells where to sit | adverb (place) |
| *Tell me **when you will come**.* | when you will come | object of *tell* | noun |
| *I remember the day **when we met**.* | when we met | describes *the day* | adjective |
| *I was sleeping **when he came**.* | when he came | tells when | adverb (time) |

**Ask:**
1. Can I replace it with *it / this*? → **noun clause**.
2. Does it come after a noun and describe that noun? → **adjective clause**.
3. Does it tell when / where / why / how / on what condition? → **adverb clause**.

## The rules, part 2: transformation of sentences (Class 11)

### Rule 9: To make a sentence simple, change the extra clauses into phrases.

Use these tools:

| Tool | Example (simple sentence) |
|---|---|
| present participle | ***Seeing** the police, the thief ran away.* |
| perfect participle | ***Having finished** the work, he went home.* |
| *to*-infinitive | *He works hard **to pass** the exam.* |
| preposition + noun / gerund | ***In spite of** his poverty, he is honest.* / ***On seeing** the police, he ran.* |
| noun in apposition | *Mr Rajan, **our principal**, gave a speech.* |
| nominative absolute (noun + participle) | ***The sun having set**, we went home.* |
| adjective or noun phrase | *The **prize-winning** student is my cousin.* |

### Rule 10: To make a sentence compound, join two main clauses with a coordinating conjunction.

| Meaning | Conjunction | Example (compound sentence) |
|---|---|---|
| addition | *and, not only ... but also, as well as* | *He saw the police **and** ran away.* |
| contrast | *but, yet, still, however* | *He is poor, **but** he is honest.* |
| choice / warning | *or, otherwise, else, either ... or* | *Work hard, **or** you will fail.* |
| result | *so, therefore, hence* | *He was ill, **so** he stayed at home.* |
| reason (formal) | *for* | *He stayed at home, **for** he was ill.* |
| negative addition | *neither ... nor, nor* | *He neither reads **nor** writes.* |

### Rule 11: To make a sentence complex, change one part into a subordinate clause.

Use a **subordinating conjunction** (for an adverb or noun clause) or a **relative pronoun** (for an adjective clause).

> *As he was ill, he stayed at home.* (adverb clause of reason)
> *If you work hard, you will pass.* (adverb clause of condition)
> *Although he is poor, he is honest.* (adverb clause of contrast)
> *The man **who won the prize** is my uncle.* (adjective clause)
> *He confessed **that he was guilty**.* (noun clause)

### Rule 12: Learn the main transformation sets, one meaning at a time.

The meaning stays the same in all three columns. Only the structure changes.

| Meaning | Simple | Compound | Complex |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reason** | ***Being** ill, he stayed at home.* / ***Because of** his illness, ...* | *He was ill, **so** he stayed at home.* | ***As** he was ill, he stayed at home.* |
| **Time** | ***On seeing** the police, the thief ran away.* / ***Seeing** the police, ...* | *The thief saw the police **and** ran away.* | ***When** the thief saw the police, he ran away.* |
| **Time (one action first)** | ***Having finished** the work, she left.* | *She finished the work **and** (then) left.* | ***After** she had finished the work, she left.* |
| **Contrast** | ***In spite of** his poverty, he is honest.* / ***Despite** being poor, ...* | *He is poor, **but** he is honest.* | ***Although** he is poor, he is honest.* |
| **Condition (positive)** | ***By working** hard, you will pass.* | *Work hard, **and** you will pass.* | ***If** you work hard, you will pass.* |
| **Condition (negative)** | ***Without working** hard, you will not pass.* | *Work hard, **or** you will fail.* | ***If** you do not work hard, you will fail.* / ***Unless** you work hard, ...* |
| **Purpose** | *He works hard **to pass** the exam.* | *He wants to pass the exam, **so** he works hard.* | *He works hard **so that** he may pass the exam.* |
| **Result (*too*)** | *He is **too** weak **to** walk.* | *He is very weak, **so** he cannot walk.* | *He is **so** weak **that** he cannot walk.* |
| **Adjective clause** | ***The winner of** the prize is my uncle.* | *The man won the prize, **and** he is my uncle.* | *The man **who won** the prize is my uncle.* |
| **Noun clause** | *He confessed **his guilt**.* | *He was guilty, **and** he confessed it.* | *He confessed **that he was guilty**.* |
| **Addition** | ***Besides** being a singer, she is a dancer.* | *She is **not only** a singer **but also** a dancer.* | *She is a dancer **who** is also a singer.* |

### Rule 13: Combine several simple sentences into one by keeping one main verb and changing the rest into phrases.

> *Ravi got up early. He took a bath. He went to the temple.*

1. Keep the last (or most important) action as the finite verb: *went*.
2. Change the earlier actions into participles: *Getting up early* / *Having taken a bath*.

> ***Getting up early and taking a bath**, Ravi went to the temple.* (simple)
> *Ravi got up early, took a bath **and** went to the temple.* (compound)
> ***After** Ravi had got up early and taken a bath, he went to the temple.* (complex)

More combining tools:

> *This is Mr Kumar. He is our maths teacher.* → *This is Mr Kumar, **our maths teacher**.* (apposition)
> *The rain stopped. We went out.* → ***The rain having stopped**, we went out.* (nominative absolute)
> *She wanted to buy a saree. She went to the shop.* → *She went to the shop **to buy** a saree.* (infinitive of purpose)

### Rule 14: Split a long sentence by turning each phrase or clause back into a sentence.

> ***Having finished** his work, Karthik, **our class leader**, went home **to rest**.*

1. *Karthik finished his work.*
2. *Karthik is our class leader.*
3. *He went home.*
4. *He wanted to rest.*

### Rule 15: Change affirmative to negative (and back) without changing the meaning.

| Affirmative | Negative | Method |
|---|---|---|
| *He is always punctual.* | *He is **never** late.* | opposite word + *not / never* |
| *She is rich.* | *She is **not poor**.* | opposite adjective + *not* |
| *Only Ravi can solve this.* | ***None but** Ravi can solve this.* | *only* (person) → *none but* |
| *He has only two pens.* | *He has **no more than** two pens.* / *He has **nothing but** two pens.* | *only* (thing / number) → *nothing but / no more than* |
| *Every mother loves her child.* | ***There is no mother who does not** love her child.* | *every* → *no ... who does not* |
| *As soon as he arrived, it rained.* | ***No sooner had** he arrived **than** it rained.* | *as soon as* → *no sooner ... than* (past perfect, inversion) |
| *Both Arun and Kavya came.* | ***Not only** Arun **but also** Kavya came.* | |
| *I was doubtful about it.* | *I was **not sure** about it.* | opposite word + *not* |
| *He is the best player.* | ***No other** player is **as good as** he.* | see [Degrees of comparison](/topics/t08-degrees-of-comparison/class-12/) |

*No sooner* is always followed by *than*, and *hardly / scarcely* by *when*: ***Hardly had** he arrived **when** it rained.*

### Rule 16: Change assertive to interrogative (and back).

A positive statement becomes a **negative question**. A negative statement becomes a **positive question**. These are **rhetorical questions**: the answer is obvious.

| Assertive | Interrogative |
|---|---|
| *Everyone knows the Taj Mahal.* | ***Who does not** know the Taj Mahal?* |
| *Nobody can do this.* | ***Who can** do this?* |
| *It is useless to cry over spilt milk.* | ***What is the use of** crying over spilt milk?* |
| *He is not honest.* | ***Is he** honest?* |
| *You should respect your parents.* | ***Shouldn't you** respect your parents?* |
| *I shall never forget this day.* | ***Shall I ever** forget this day?* |
| *There is no one in the room.* | ***Is there** anyone in the room?* |

### Rule 17: Change exclamatory to assertive (and back).

| Exclamatory | Assertive |
|---|---|
| *What a beautiful flower it is!* | *It is a **very** beautiful flower.* |
| *How tall he is!* | *He is **very** tall.* |
| *How kind of you!* | *It is **very kind** of you.* |
| *Alas! He failed.* | ***It is sad that** he failed.* / *I am sorry that he failed.* |
| *Hurray! We won.* | ***It is a matter of great joy that** we won.* |
| *If only I were young again!* | ***I wish** I were young again.* |
| *What a fool I have been!* | *I have been **a great fool**.* |

Going back: *very* + adjective → *What a* + adjective + noun + subject + verb*!* or *How* + adjective + subject + verb*!*

### Rule 18: Remove *too* by using *so ... that ... not* (and add it back).

*Too ... to* has a **negative** meaning: *He is too weak to walk* = he cannot walk. When you remove *too*, the negative must appear.

| With *too* (simple) | Without *too* (complex) |
|---|---|
| *He is **too** weak **to** walk.* | *He is **so** weak **that** he **cannot** walk.* |
| *The box is **too** heavy **for** me **to** lift.* | *The box is **so** heavy **that** I **cannot** lift it.* |
| *She was **too** tired **to** work.* | *She was **so** tired **that** she **could not** work.* |
| *The tea is **too** hot **to** drink.* | *The tea is **so** hot **that** it **cannot** be drunk.* / *... that we cannot drink it.* |

Notice:
- Past tense → *could not*.
- Add the object pronoun (*it*) in the *that* clause when the verb needs an object: *heavy that I cannot lift **it***.

The positive version is *enough ... to* ↔ *so ... that ... can*: *He is rich **enough to** buy a car.* → *He is **so** rich **that** he **can** buy a car.*

## Special cases

### 1. *That* left out of a noun or adjective clause

> *I hope **(that)** you are well.* (still a noun clause)
> *The pen **(that)** I bought is blue.* (still an adjective clause)

Count the finite verbs: there are still two clauses.

### 2. A conjunction joining two verbs, not two clauses

> *Ravi came and sat down.*

Many school grammars call this **compound**, because there are two finite verbs (*Ravi came* + *[he] sat down*). Others call it a **simple** sentence with one subject and two verbs. Follow the method your textbook uses. If an exam asks you to count finite verbs, count two.

### 3. *Since* and *as*: time or reason

> ***Since** it is late, let's go home.* (reason)
> *I have been here **since** I was a child.* (time)
> ***As** I was walking, I saw a snake.* (time)
> ***As** he was ill, he stayed home.* (reason)

### 4. *While*: time or contrast

> ***While** I was cooking, the phone rang.* (time)
> ***While** Ravi likes cricket, his sister likes chess.* (contrast)

### 5. *Unless* already contains *not*

*Unless* = *if ... not*. Do not add another *not*:

> ***Unless** you hurry, you will miss the bus.* ✔
> *~~Unless you do not hurry, ...~~* ✘

See [Conditional clauses (Class 12)](/topics/t13-conditionals/class-12/).

### 6. Changing the tense when making a participle clause

When the first action is clearly complete, use the **perfect participle**: *He had finished the work. He went home.* → ***Having finished** the work, he went home.* Do not write *~~Finishing the work, he went home~~* if the order matters.

### 7. Keep the same subject when using a participle

*~~Being a holiday, the shops were closed.~~* ✘ → ***It being** a holiday, the shops were closed.* ✔ / ***As it was** a holiday, the shops were closed.* ✔

(The shops were not "a holiday". See the dangling participle in [Non-finite verbs](/topics/t07-non-finite-verbs/class-12/).)

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Although he is poor, **~~but~~** he is honest.* | *Although he is poor, he is honest.* / *He is poor, but he is honest.* | Use one joining word only. *Although* and *but* together is a double conjunction. |
| *Because he was ill, **~~so~~** he stayed home.* | *Because he was ill, he stayed home.* | Same problem: *because* and *so* together. |
| *Unless you do **~~not~~** work hard, you will fail.* | *Unless you work hard, you will fail.* | *Unless* already means *if not*. |
| *He is **~~too weak that~~** he cannot walk.* | *He is **so** weak that he cannot walk.* | *Too* goes with *to*; *so* goes with *that*. |
| *The box is so heavy that I cannot lift.* | *... that I cannot lift **it**.* | *Lift* needs an object in the *that* clause. |
| *No sooner had he arrived **~~when~~** it rained.* | *No sooner had he arrived **than** it rained.* | *No sooner* takes *than*. (*Hardly* takes *when*.) |
| *~~Being a rainy day~~, we stayed in.* | ***It being** a rainy day, we stayed in.* / ***As it was** a rainy day, ...* | The participle needs the right subject (*it*, not *we*). |
| *The man **~~which~~** came here is my uncle.* | *The man **who** came here is my uncle.* | *Who* for people, *which* for things. |
| *My father, **~~that~~** is a doctor, lives in Salem.* | *My father, **who** is a doctor, lives in Salem.* | No *that* in a non-defining clause (with commas). |
| *I know that he is honest.* → "compound" | **complex** | *That he is honest* is a noun clause (a subordinate clause). *That* is a subordinating word, not a coordinating one. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Identify the kind of sentence** (simple, compound, complex; or assertive, interrogative...).
- **Pick out the finite verbs.**
- **Mark the main and subordinate clauses**, and **name the subordinate clause** (noun, adjective, adverb of time / reason...).
- **Do as directed**: change into simple / compound / complex; change into negative / interrogative / assertive; remove *too*.
- **Combine** two or three sentences into one.

### Steps for "Identify the kind of sentence (structure)"

1. Underline every **finite verb**.
2. One finite verb → **simple**.
3. Two or more: look at the **joining words**.
   - Only *and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor* (or a semicolon) → **compound**.
   - Any subordinating word (*when, because, if, although, that, who, which...*) → **complex**.

### Steps for "Change the kind of sentence"

1. **Find the relationship** between the two ideas: reason, time, contrast, condition, purpose, result, description (adjective clause) or fact (noun clause).
2. **Find the row** in the transformation table (Rule 12).
3. **Choose the tool** for the target kind: phrase (simple), coordinating conjunction (compound), subordinating word (complex).
4. **Change the verb form** if needed (*was ill → being ill*; *his illness*).
5. **Check**: same meaning? only one joining word? same subject for participles?

### Worked example 1

> *Identify the kind and the clauses: The boy who broke the window ran away when he saw the owner.*

1. Finite verbs: *broke, ran, saw* → 3 clauses.
2. Joining words: *who, when* → subordinating → **complex**.
3. Main clause: *The boy ran away*.
4. *who broke the window*: describes *the boy* → **adjective clause**.
5. *when he saw the owner*: tells when → **adverb clause of time**.

**Answer:** **Complex.** Main: *The boy ... ran away*. Subordinate: *who broke the window* (adjective) and *when he saw the owner* (adverb, time).

### Worked example 2

> *Change into complex and simple: Meena was tired, so she went to bed early.*

1. Kind given: compound (*so*). Relationship: **reason**.
2. Complex: reason → *As / Because / Since* + clause.
3. Simple: reason → participle *Being* or *Because of* + noun.

**Answer:**
- **Complex:** ***As** Meena was tired, she went to bed early.*
- **Simple:** ***Being** tired, Meena went to bed early.* (or ***Because of** her tiredness, ...*)

### Worked example 3

> *Change into compound and complex: In spite of his hard work, he failed.*

1. Kind given: simple. Relationship: **contrast**.
2. Compound: *but*. Complex: *although*.
3. *His hard work* → clause: *he worked hard*.

**Answer:**
- **Compound:** *He worked hard, **but** he failed.*
- **Complex:** ***Although** he worked hard, he failed.*

### Worked example 4

> *Remove "too": The question was too difficult for the students to answer.*

1. *too ... for ... to* → *so ... that ... not*.
2. Past tense (*was*) → *could not*.
3. *Answer* needs an object → *it*.

**Answer:** *The question was **so** difficult **that** the students **could not** answer **it**.*

### Worked example 5

> *Combine into a simple sentence: The sun rose. The fog disappeared.*

1. The two subjects are **different** (*the sun, the fog*). A normal participle would dangle.
2. Use the **nominative absolute**: noun + participle.
3. Or use a prepositional phrase of time.

**Answer:** ***The sun having risen**, the fog disappeared.* (or ***At sunrise**, the fog disappeared.*)

### Worked example 6

> *Change into interrogative: Nobody can stop him.*

1. Negative statement → positive question.
2. *Nobody can* → *Who can*?

**Answer:** ***Who can** stop him?*

## Quick check

1. Simple, compound or complex? *If you need help, call me.*
2. Name the clause: *I don't know **why she is crying**.*
3. Change into compound: *Although Arun is young, he is very wise.*
4. Remove *too*: *He is too poor to buy a car.*
5. Change into assertive: *What a lovely garden this is!*

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **Complex.** Two finite verbs (*need, call*), joined by *if*, a subordinating conjunction.
2. **Noun clause** (object of *know*). Test: *I don't know **it**.* ✔
3. ***Arun is young, but he is very wise.*** Contrast → *but*.
4. ***He is so poor that he cannot buy a car.*** *Too ... to* → *so ... that ... cannot*.
5. ***This is a very lovely garden.*** *What a* + adjective → *very* + adjective. (*This is a very beautiful garden* is also fine.)

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **By function:** assertive · interrogative · imperative · exclamatory (· optative).
- **By structure:** simple (1 finite verb) · compound (main + main, with *and / but / or / so*) · complex (main + subordinate).
- **Count finite verbs = count clauses.**
- **Noun clause** → replace with *it*. **Adjective clause** → after a noun, *who / which / that*. **Adverb clause** → when, where, why, how, condition, contrast, purpose, result.
- **Transform:** simple = participle / infinitive / phrase · compound = coordinating conjunction · complex = subordinating word or relative pronoun.
- **One joining word only:** never *although ... but*, *because ... so*.
- *too ... to* ↔ *so ... that ... not* · *as soon as* ↔ *no sooner ... than* · *only* ↔ *none but*.
:::
