## What you will learn

- The difference between a **finite** verb and a **non-finite** verb
- The **infinitive**:
  - the *to*-infinitive as subject, object and complement, and to show purpose (*He came **to help***)
  - the bare infinitive after modals, *make, let, help, see, hear*
  - verbs followed by a *to*-infinitive: *decide, hope, refuse, agree, promise...*
- The **gerund** (V4 used as a noun): as subject, object and after prepositions; verbs followed by a gerund: *enjoy, avoid, mind, finish, suggest, deny...*
- The **participles**:
  - present participle: *a **running** stream*, ***Seeing** the police, the thief ran away*
  - past participle: *a **broken** chair*
  - perfect participle: ***Having finished** the work, he left*
- How to tell a **gerund** from a **present participle** (same *-ing* form, different job)
- Verbs whose meaning changes: *remember, forget, stop, try, regret*
- The **dangling participle** error: *~~Walking along the road, a snake bit him.~~*
- How to answer "Identify", "Fill in" and "Combine using a participle"

## Why this matters

Non-finite verbs let us say more in fewer words. Compare:

> *The thief saw the police. He ran away.*
> ***Seeing** the police, the thief ran away.*

The second sentence is shorter and smoother. Good writers use non-finite verbs all the time.

In the exam, you will identify non-finite verbs, fill in the right form (*to go* or *going*?), and combine sentences using participles. This topic also connects with [transformation of sentences](/topics/t09-kinds-of-sentences/kinds-of-sentences/), where we use participles and infinitives to make a sentence simple.

## The idea in simple words

Look at the verbs in this sentence.

> *Ravi **wants** **to learn** **swimming**.*

- *wants* changes with time and subject: *Ravi wanted, they want*. It is a **finite** verb.
- *to learn* never changes: *Ravi wanted **to learn**, they want **to learn***. It is a **non-finite** verb (an **infinitive**).
- *swimming* works like a noun here: it is the thing Ravi wants to learn. It is also **non-finite** (a **gerund**).

Now this one:

> *The **crying** baby woke up everyone.*

*Crying* describes the baby, like an adjective. It is a **participle**.

So there are three kinds of non-finite verbs:

| Kind | Form | Works like |
|---|---|---|
| Infinitive | *to* + V1 (or V1 alone) | a noun, an adjective or an adverb |
| Gerund | V4 (*-ing*) | a noun |
| Participle | V4 (*-ing*), V3, or *having* + V3 | an adjective (or a short clause) |

## Grammar words you need

**Finite verb:** a verb that shows tense and changes with the subject: *She **writes**. They **write**. She **wrote**.* Every clause has one. See [Phrase and clause](/foundation/f5-phrase-clause/).

**Non-finite verb:** a verb form that does **not** show tense and does **not** change with the subject. It cannot be the only verb in a full sentence.

**Infinitive:** the base form of a verb, usually with *to*: *to eat, to go*. Without *to*, it is the **bare infinitive**: *eat, go*.

**Gerund:** the V4 (*-ing*) form used as a **noun**: ***Swimming** is fun.*

**Participle:** a verb form used as an **adjective**, or to make a short clause. Present participle: V4 (*running*). Past participle: V3 (*broken*). Perfect participle: *having* + V3 (*having finished*).

**Participle clause:** a group of words that starts with a participle and works like a short clause: ***Hearing the noise**, she woke up.*

## The rules

### Rule 1: A finite verb shows tense and agrees with the subject. A non-finite verb does neither.

| Finite | Non-finite |
|---|---|
| *She **sings**.* / *She **sang**.* / *They **sing**.* | *She likes **to sing**.* / *She liked **to sing**.* / *They like **to sing**.* |
| *He **is playing**.* | ***Playing** cricket is fun.* |
| *The chair **was broken**.* | *a **broken** chair* |

**Test:** change the subject or the tense. If the verb changes, it is finite. If it stays the same, it is non-finite.

### Rule 2: The *to*-infinitive can be the subject, the object or the complement of a verb.

> ***To err** is human.* (subject)
> *It is easy **to learn** Tamil.* (real subject, with *it* in front)
> *I want **to become** a doctor.* (object of *want*)
> *Her dream is **to visit** Kashmir.* (complement, after *is*)

The *to*-infinitive also follows:

- **adjectives:** *I am happy **to help**.* / *The question is difficult **to answer**.*
- **nouns:** *She made a decision **to leave**.* / *I have no time **to waste**.*
- **question words:** *I don't know **how to swim**.* / *Tell me **what to do**.*

### Rule 3: Use the *to*-infinitive to show purpose (why someone does something).

> *Arun went to the market **to buy** vegetables.* (Why did he go? To buy vegetables.)
> *She studies hard **to get** a scholarship.*
> *We came **to see** you.*

For a stronger or more formal purpose, use ***in order to*** or ***so as to***:

> *He left early **in order to** catch the bus.*
> *She whispered **so as not to** wake the baby.* (negative purpose)

### Rule 4: Use the bare infinitive (no *to*) after modals, *make, let, help*, and verbs of seeing and hearing.

| After | Example |
|---|---|
| modals (*can, will, must, should...*) | *You **must go**.* |
| *make* (= force) | *The teacher **made** us **rewrite** the essay.* |
| *let* (= allow) | *Please **let** me **go**.* |
| *help* (*to* is optional) | *She **helped** me **(to) carry** the bags.* |
| *see, hear, watch, feel, notice* | *I **saw** him **cross** the road.* / *We **heard** her **sing**.* |
| *had better, would rather* | *You **had better leave**.* / *I **would rather stay**.* |

In the **passive**, *make* and *see / hear* take *to*:

> *We **were made to rewrite** the essay.*
> *He **was seen to enter** the house.*

See / hear + **bare infinitive** = the whole action. See / hear + **V4** = part of an action in progress:

> *I saw him **cross** the road.* (from one side to the other)
> *I saw him **crossing** the road.* (in the middle of crossing)

### Rule 5: Some verbs are followed by a *to*-infinitive.

> *Kavya **decided to study** medicine.*
> *He **refused to answer**.*
> *We **hope to win** the match.*

| Verb + *to*-infinitive |
|---|
| *agree, afford, aim, appear, arrange, ask, attempt, choose, decide, deserve, expect, fail, hope, learn, manage, offer, plan, prepare, pretend, promise, refuse, seem, tend, threaten, want, wish, would like* |

Some verbs take **an object** and then the *to*-infinitive:

> *I **asked him to wait**.*
> *The doctor **advised me to rest**.*
> *They **allowed us to leave** early.*

| Verb + object + *to*-infinitive |
|---|
| *advise, allow, ask, encourage, expect, force, invite, order, permit, persuade, remind, teach, tell, want, warn* |

### Rule 6: The gerund works as a noun: as subject, object, complement, and after prepositions.

> ***Swimming** is good exercise.* (subject)
> *I enjoy **reading**.* (object of *enjoy*)
> *Her hobby is **painting**.* (complement)
> *He is fond **of reading**.* (after a preposition)
> *She left **without saying** goodbye.* (after a preposition)
> *Thank you **for helping** me.* (after a preposition)

**After every preposition, use a gerund, not an infinitive:**

> *~~He is good at to sing.~~* ✘ → *He is good **at singing**.* ✔

When *to* is a **preposition** (not part of an infinitive), it is also followed by a gerund:

> *I look forward **to meeting** you.*
> *She is used **to getting** up early.*
> *He objected **to being** called lazy.*

**Test:** can you put a noun after the *to*? *I look forward to **the holidays*** ✔ → *to* is a preposition → gerund.

### Rule 7: Some verbs are followed by a gerund.

> *I **enjoy listening** to music.*
> *He **avoided meeting** me.*
> *Would you **mind closing** the window?*
> *She **finished writing** the letter.*
> *Ravi **suggested going** to the beach.*
> *The man **denied stealing** the money.*

| Verb + gerund |
|---|
| *admit, avoid, can't help, can't stand, consider, delay, deny, dislike, enjoy, finish, give up, imagine, keep (on), mind, miss, postpone, practise, put off, quit, recommend, risk, suggest* |

*~~He suggested to go~~* ✘ → *He suggested **going*** ✔ / *He suggested **that we should go*** ✔

### Rule 8: The present participle (V4) works as an adjective, or starts a participle clause.

**As an adjective** (it shows something that is doing the action):

> *a **running** stream* (a stream that runs)
> *a **sleeping** baby* (a baby that is sleeping)
> *the **rising** sun*
> *an **interesting** film* (a film that interests people)

**In a participle clause** (instead of a full clause with *when, because, as*):

> ***Seeing** the police, the thief ran away.* (= When / Because he saw the police...)
> ***Hearing** the bell, the students went to class.*
> ***Being** tired, she went to bed early.* (= As she was tired...)

**After some verbs**, to describe how someone did something:

> *She came **running**.* / *He sat **reading** a book.* / *They stood **waiting** at the gate.*

### Rule 9: The past participle (V3) works as an adjective, usually with a passive meaning.

> *a **broken** chair* (a chair that has been broken)
> *the **stolen** money* (money that was stolen)
> ***fallen** leaves*
> *a **well-known** writer*
> *a **bored** student* (a student who feels bored)

**In a participle clause** (passive meaning):

> ***Written** in simple English, the book is easy to read.* (= Because it is written...)
> ***Shocked** by the news, she sat down.*
> ***Built** in the 12th century, the temple is very old.*

**In *have something done*** (someone else does it for you):

> *I **had** my hair **cut**.* / *We **got** the car **repaired**.*

### Rule 10: The perfect participle (*having* + V3) shows that one action was completed before another.

> ***Having finished** the work, he went home.* (First he finished. Then he went.)
> ***Having lost** all his money, he could not buy a ticket.*
> ***Having been warned** about the rain, we took umbrellas.* (passive: *having been* + V3)

Use the perfect participle when the first action must be complete before the second. When the actions are close together, the present participle is enough: ***Opening** the door, she saw her friend.*

Negative: ***Not having** studied*, *he failed.* (*Not* goes before the participle.)

### Rule 11: A gerund and a present participle look the same. Tell them apart by their job.

Both end in *-ing*. The **gerund** is a noun. The **participle** is an adjective or part of a verb.

| Sentence | *-ing* word | Job | Kind |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Walking** is good exercise.* | walking | subject (a noun) | gerund |
| *I saw a **walking** robot.* | walking | describes *robot* (a robot that walks) | participle |
| *a **swimming** pool* | swimming | a pool **for** swimming (the purpose) | gerund |
| *a **swimming** girl* | swimming | a girl **who is** swimming | participle |
| *a **walking** stick* | walking | a stick **for** walking | gerund |
| *She is **singing**.* | singing | part of the verb *is singing* | participle |

**Tests:**
1. Can you replace it with a noun or *it*? (*Walking is good* → *It is good*.) → gerund.
2. Does it mean "for doing" (*a sleeping bag* = a bag for sleeping)? → gerund.
3. Does it mean "which / who is doing" (*a sleeping baby* = a baby who is sleeping)? → participle.

### Rule 12: Some verbs change their meaning with a gerund or an infinitive.

| Verb | + *to*-infinitive | + gerund |
|---|---|---|
| **remember** | an action you must do (after remembering) *Remember **to lock** the door.* | a memory of a past action *I remember **locking** the door.* (I locked it, and I remember that.) |
| **forget** | not do something you should do *I forgot **to call** her.* (I didn't call.) | not remember a past action *I'll never forget **meeting** the Chief Minister.* |
| **stop** | stop one thing in order to do another *He stopped **to drink** water.* (He stopped walking so that he could drink.) | end an activity *He stopped **drinking** coffee.* (He no longer drinks it.) |
| **try** | make an effort (it may be hard) *I tried **to lift** the box, but it was too heavy.* | do something as a test or experiment *Try **adding** some salt. It may taste better.* |
| **regret** | be sorry to say something now (formal) *We regret **to inform** you that the train is late.* | be sorry about a past action *I regret **shouting** at him.* |

Two more:

> *go on **to do*** (do something new next) / *go on **doing*** (continue the same thing)
> *mean **to do*** (intend) / *mean **doing*** (involve): *This job **means travelling** a lot.*

Some verbs take either form with **little or no change** in meaning: *begin, start, continue, like, love, hate, prefer*.

> *It began **to rain**.* = *It began **raining**.*

### Rule 13: The subject of a participle clause must be the subject of the main clause. Otherwise, it is a dangling participle.

A participle clause has no subject of its own. English assumes that its subject is the **subject of the main clause**.

> *~~Walking along the road, a snake bit him.~~* ✘

This says the **snake** was walking along the road. That is wrong.

> ***Walking** along the road, **he** was bitten by a snake.* ✔
> *While **he** was walking along the road, a snake bit him.* ✔

More examples:

| Wrong (dangling) | Right |
|---|---|
| *~~Entering the room, the lights were switched on.~~* | *Entering the room, **she** switched on the lights.* |
| *~~Having finished dinner, the TV was switched on.~~* | *Having finished dinner, **we** switched on the TV.* |
| *~~Being a rainy day, I stayed at home.~~* | ***It being** a rainy day, I stayed at home.* / ***As it was** a rainy day, I stayed at home.* |

## Special cases

### 1. The negative infinitive: *not to*

> *He promised **not to be** late.* (not *~~to not be~~* in formal English)
> *The doctor told me **not to eat** sweets.*

### 2. Passive and perfect infinitives

> *The work is **to be done** today.* (passive infinitive: *to be* + V3)
> *She seems **to have forgotten** us.* (perfect infinitive: *to have* + V3, an earlier action)
> *He is said **to have been** a great king.*

### 3. Passive gerund: *being* + V3

> *She hates **being** **called** by her nickname.*
> *He was afraid of **being punished**.*

See [Active and passive voice (Class 12)](/topics/t11-active-passive/class-12/).

### 4. *Need* + V4 has a passive meaning

> *The car **needs washing**.* = *The car needs **to be washed**.*

### 5. *-ing* and *-ed* adjectives

> *The film was **boring**.* (it made people feel bored)
> *I was **bored**.* (how I felt)
> *an **interesting** lesson / an **interested** student*
> *a **frightening** story / a **frightened** child*

*-ing* = the thing that causes the feeling. *-ed* = the person who has the feeling.

### 6. *Too ... to* and *enough ... to*

> *He is **too** weak **to walk**.* (He cannot walk.)
> *She is old **enough to vote**.*

See [Kinds of sentences: removing *too*](/topics/t09-kinds-of-sentences/kinds-of-sentences/).

### 7. A possessive or object before a gerund

> *I don't mind **his** coming late.* (formal)
> *I don't mind **him** coming late.* (common)

Both are correct. The possessive is more formal.

### 8. The split infinitive

*To clearly explain* puts a word between *to* and the verb. Older grammar books called this wrong. Today it is accepted, especially when moving the adverb would sound awkward. In formal writing, *to explain clearly* is still the safer choice.

### 9. Fixed expressions with a gerund

> ***It is no use** crying over spilt milk.*
> ***It is worth** visiting Rameswaram.*
> ***There is no point in** waiting.*
> *I **can't help** laughing.*

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *I enjoy ~~to read~~.* | *I enjoy **reading**.* | *Enjoy* is followed by a gerund. |
| *He suggested ~~to go~~ to the beach.* | *He suggested **going** to the beach.* | *Suggest* takes a gerund (or a *that* clause). |
| *She decided ~~going~~ home.* | *She decided **to go** home.* | *Decide* takes a *to*-infinitive. |
| *He is fond of ~~to swim~~.* | *He is fond of **swimming**.* | After a preposition, use a gerund. |
| *I look forward to ~~meet~~ you.* | *I look forward to **meeting** you.* | *To* here is a preposition → gerund. |
| *The teacher made us ~~to stand~~.* | *The teacher made us **stand**.* | *Make* (active) + bare infinitive. |
| *Let me ~~to go~~.* | *Let me **go**.* | *Let* + bare infinitive. |
| *~~Walking along the road, a snake bit him.~~* | *Walking along the road, **he** was bitten by a snake.* | Dangling participle: the snake was not walking. |
| *I am ~~interesting~~ in music.* | *I am **interested** in music.* | The person who has the feeling → *-ed*. |
| *Would you mind ~~to open~~ the door?* | *Would you mind **opening** the door?* | *Mind* takes a gerund. |
| *He avoided ~~to meet~~ me.* | *He avoided **meeting** me.* | *Avoid* takes a gerund. |
| *She stopped ~~to eat~~ chocolate because of her teeth.* | *She stopped **eating** chocolate...* | She ended the habit → *stop* + gerund. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Identify** the non-finite verb and its type (infinitive, gerund, participle).
- **Fill in** with the correct form: *to*-infinitive, bare infinitive, gerund or participle.
- **Combine** two sentences using a participle (or an infinitive).

### Steps for "Identify the non-finite verb"

1. **Find all the verbs.** Mark the finite verb (it shows tense).
2. **Look at the others:**
   - *to* + V1 (or V1 after *make, let*, a modal) → **infinitive**.
   - *-ing* word used as a noun (subject, object, after a preposition) → **gerund**.
   - *-ing* word describing a noun, or starting a clause about the subject → **present participle**.
   - V3 describing a noun → **past participle**.
   - *having* + V3 → **perfect participle**.

### Steps for "Combine using a participle"

1. **Find the two sentences** and check they have the **same subject**.
2. **Choose the action that happened first** (or the reason). That one becomes the participle clause.
3. **Choose the participle:**
   - the two actions are close together → present participle (V4): *Seeing...*
   - the first action is complete before the second → perfect participle: *Having seen...*
   - the subject receives the action (passive) → past participle (V3): *Shocked by...*, or *Having been* + V3.
4. **Remove the subject and the finite verb** from that sentence. Keep the subject in the main clause.
5. **Check for a dangling participle**: the main clause must start with the same subject.

### Worked example 1

> *Combine using a participle: The boy saw the snake. He ran away.*

1. Same subject: *the boy* / *he*. ✔
2. First action / reason: *saw the snake*.
3. Close together → present participle: *Seeing the snake*.
4. Main clause: *the boy ran away*.

**Answer:** ***Seeing the snake**, the boy ran away.*

### Worked example 2

> *Combine using a participle: She finished her homework. She went out to play.*

1. Same subject: *she*. ✔
2. First action: *finished her homework*. It must be complete before she goes out.
3. Perfect participle: *Having finished her homework*.

**Answer:** ***Having finished her homework**, she went out to play.*

### Worked example 3

> *Combine using a participle: The temple was built in the 10th century. It attracts many tourists.*

1. Same subject: *the temple* / *it*. ✔
2. The temple received the action (*was built*) → passive → past participle.
3. *Built in the 10th century*.

**Answer:** ***Built in the 10th century**, the temple attracts many tourists.*

### Worked example 4

> *Identify the non-finite verbs and their types: Having lost his ticket, Ravi decided to walk home instead of waiting for the bus.*

1. Finite verb: *decided* (past tense).
2. *Having lost* → **perfect participle**.
3. *to walk* → **to-infinitive** (object of *decided*).
4. *waiting* → **gerund** (after the preposition *of*).

### Worked example 5

> *Fill in: I remember ______ (visit) Kanyakumari when I was six.*

1. *Remember* + a memory of a past action → gerund.

**Answer:** *I remember **visiting** Kanyakumari when I was six.*

## Quick check

1. Gerund or participle? *I need a **sleeping** bag.*
2. *She refused ______ (answer) my question.*
3. *My mother let me ______ (watch) the late-night match.*
4. Combine using a participle: *He was tired. He went to bed early.*
5. Correct the error: *Opening the door, the cat ran out.* (Meaning: I opened the door, and the cat ran out.)

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. **Gerund.** A *sleeping bag* is a bag **for** sleeping, not a bag that is sleeping.
2. ***to answer.*** *Refuse* is followed by a *to*-infinitive.
3. ***watch.*** *Let* is followed by a bare infinitive (no *to*).
4. ***Being tired, he went to bed early.*** The reason becomes a present participle clause. (*Feeling tired* is also good.)
5. ***When I opened the door, the cat ran out.*** (or *Opening the door, I saw the cat run out*.) In the original, the participle has no correct subject: the cat did not open the door.

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **Finite** verbs show tense and agree with the subject. **Non-finite** verbs (infinitive, gerund, participle) do not.
- ***to*-infinitive**: subject, object, complement, purpose. **Bare infinitive** after modals, *make, let, help, see, hear*.
- **Gerund** = *-ing* as a noun. Always after prepositions (*fond of reading, look forward to meeting*).
- *enjoy, avoid, mind, finish, suggest, deny* + gerund. *decide, hope, refuse, agree, promise* + *to*-infinitive.
- **Participles**: present (*a running stream*), past (*a broken chair*), perfect (*Having finished, he left*).
- *remember / forget / stop / try / regret* change meaning: *to* = future or purpose; *-ing* = past or the activity itself.
- A participle clause must share the **subject** of the main clause. No dangling participles.
:::
