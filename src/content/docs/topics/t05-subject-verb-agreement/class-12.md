## What you will learn

- The basic rule: a **singular subject** takes a **singular verb**, a **plural subject** takes a **plural verb**
- How to find the **real subject** when other words come between it and the verb
- Every special rule:
  - subjects joined by *and*, and when *and* still takes a singular verb (*Bread and butter **is***)
  - *either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also*
  - *with, along with, as well as, together with*
  - *each, every, everyone, nobody, either, neither*
  - collective nouns (*team, family, jury*)
  - *the number of* and *a number of*
  - *one of the* and *one of those who*
  - nouns that look plural (*news, mathematics*) and nouns that are always plural (*scissors, cattle, police*)
  - amounts, titles, and *there is / there are*
- How to answer "Choose the correct verb", "Fill in with is / are, has / have" and error spotting

## Why this matters

*The list of names **is** ready* or *The list of names **are** ready*? Most people get this wrong in a hurry. Agreement errors are some of the easiest errors to spot in an exam, and they are also very common in letters and essays.

This topic is tested every year: "Choose the correct verb", "Fill in with *is / are*", and error spotting. It also helps with [Articles and determiners](/topics/t03-articles-determiners/class-12/) (*each, every, either*) and [Kinds of sentences](/topics/t09-kinds-of-sentences/kinds-of-sentences/).

## The idea in simple words

Look at these pairs.

> *The boy **plays**.* / *The boys **play**.*
> *My sister **is** a doctor.* / *My sisters **are** doctors.*
> *Ravi **has** a cycle.* / *Ravi and Arun **have** cycles.*

When the subject is **one** person or thing, the verb is singular (*plays, is, has*). When the subject is **more than one**, the verb is plural (*play, are, have*). The subject and the verb must "agree", like two friends who always wear the same colour.

Notice something strange: in the present tense, a **singular** verb often ends in *-s* (*plays*), but a **plural** noun ends in *-s* (*boys*). So *-s* on the noun usually means **no** *-s* on the verb.

## Grammar words you need

**Subject:** the person or thing that does the action or that the sentence is about. See [Subject, verb, object and complement](/foundation/f4-sentence-parts/).

**Agreement (concord):** the rule that the verb must match the subject in **number** (singular or plural) and **person** (*I, you, he...*).

**Singular verb:** *is, was, has, does*, and V5 (*plays, goes, writes*).

**Plural verb:** *are, were, have, do*, and V1 (*play, go, write*).

**Collective noun:** a noun for a group: *team, family, class, jury, committee, audience, crowd, government*.

**Head noun:** the main noun in a long subject. In *The box of mangoes*, the head noun is *box*.

## The rules

### Rule 1: A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb.

| Subject | Singular verb | Plural verb |
|---|---|---|
| present simple | *She **writes**.* | *They **write**.* |
| *be* (present) | *He **is** ready.* | *We **are** ready.* |
| *be* (past) | *It **was** hot.* | *They **were** hot.* |
| *have* | *Kavya **has** finished.* | *The girls **have** finished.* |
| *do* | *He **doesn't** know.* | *They **don't** know.* |

Only these forms change. The past simple (*wrote*) and modals (*can, will, must*) are the same for all subjects.

*I* and *you* take plural forms: *I **am** / I **have** / I **go***; *You **are** / you **have** / you **go***, even when *you* means one person.

### Rule 2: Find the real subject. Ignore the words between the subject and the verb.

A phrase can come between the subject and the verb. The verb agrees with the **head noun**, not with the noun nearest to it.

> *The **box** of mangoes **is** heavy.* (not *~~are~~*: the box is heavy)
> *The **quality** of these mangoes **is** excellent.*
> *The **students** in my class **are** very friendly.*
> *A **bouquet** of roses **was** given to the chief guest.*
> *The **list** of names **is** on the notice board.*

**Tip:** cover the *of...* phrase with your finger. *The box ~~of mangoes~~ **is** heavy.*

### Rule 3: Two subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb.

> ***Ravi and Priya are** cousins.*
> ***My father and my uncle work** in Coimbatore.*
> ***Rice and wheat are** grown in Tamil Nadu.*

### Rule 4: Two nouns joined by *and* take a singular verb when they mean one idea, one thing or one person.

> ***Bread and butter is** my breakfast.* (one dish)
> ***Idli and sambar is** a popular breakfast in Chennai.* (one meal)
> ***Rice and curry is** served at noon.*
> ***Slow and steady wins** the race.* (one idea)
> ***The secretary and treasurer has** arrived.* (one person with two jobs)

Compare:

> ***The secretary and the treasurer have** arrived.* (two people: *the* is repeated)

When *the* (or *a, my*) is used **once**, it is one person. When it is **repeated**, they are two people.

Other common pairs that take a singular verb: *law and order, time and tide, the long and short of it, horse and carriage, rice and dal*.

> ***Law and order is** the duty of the police.*

### Rule 5: *Each* or *every* before nouns joined by *and* makes the verb singular.

> ***Every** boy and **every** girl **was** given a prize.*
> ***Each** teacher and **each** student **has** a role.*

### Rule 6: With *either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also, or* and *nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject.

> ***Either** Ravi **or** his brothers **are** coming.* (*brothers* is nearer → plural)
> ***Either** his brothers **or** Ravi **is** coming.* (*Ravi* is nearer → singular)
> ***Neither** the teacher **nor** the students **were** present.*
> ***Not only** the students **but also** the teacher **was** happy.*
> ***Neither** you **nor** I **am** responsible.* (*I* is nearer → *am*)

When both subjects are singular, the verb is singular:

> ***Either** Meena **or** Divya **has** the key.*

**Tip:** when one subject is singular and one is plural, put the **plural one last**. The sentence sounds more natural: *Neither the captain nor the players **were** happy.*

### Rule 7: With *with, along with, as well as, together with, in addition to, like, besides* and *including*, the verb agrees with the first subject.

These words do **not** join two subjects. They add extra information. The real subject is the first one.

> ***The teacher**, along with the students, **is** going on a trip.*
> ***Ravi**, as well as his friends, **plays** cricket.*
> ***The minister**, with his staff, **has** arrived.*
> ***The house**, together with its garden, **was** sold.*
> ***My brother**, like my father, **is** a doctor.*

Compare *and* (two subjects, plural) with *as well as* (one subject, singular):

> ***Ravi and his friends play** cricket.*
> ***Ravi as well as his friends plays** cricket.*

### Rule 8: *Each, every, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, anyone, nobody, no one, nothing, either, neither* take a singular verb.

> ***Each** of the students **has** a new book.*
> ***Everyone is** here.*
> ***Somebody has** taken my pen.*
> ***Nobody knows** the answer.*
> ***Either** of the roads **leads** to Salem.*
> ***Neither** of my sisters **is** married.*

*Each of, either of, neither of* + plural noun still take a **singular** verb in formal English. (*Neither of them **are*** is common in speech, but use the singular in exams.)

### Rule 9: A collective noun takes a singular verb when the group acts as one unit, and a plural verb when the members act separately.

> ***The team is** very strong this year.* (the team as one unit)
> ***The team are** arguing among themselves.* (the members, each with an opinion)
> ***The family is** large.* (one unit)
> ***The family are** having their dinner.* (each member eats)
> ***The jury has** given its verdict.* (one decision)
> ***The jury are** divided in their opinions.* (members disagree)

**Clues for plural:** *among themselves, their, divided, disagree, each with his / her own*.

**British and American English:** British English often uses a plural verb with collective nouns (*The government **are***). American English usually uses a singular verb (*The government **is***). Both are accepted. In exams, use the singular unless the sentence clearly shows the members acting separately.

Make the pronoun match too: *The committee **has** made **its** decision.* / *The committee **have** made **their** decisions.*

### Rule 10: *The number of* takes a singular verb. *A number of* takes a plural verb.

> ***The number of** students in our school **is** 1,200.* (one number)
> ***A number of** students **were** absent today.* (= many students)

**Tip:** *The number* = one figure → singular. *A number of* = *many* → plural.

### Rule 11: *One of the* + plural noun takes a singular verb. *One of those who* takes a plural verb.

> ***One** of the boys **was** hurt.* (one boy was hurt)
> ***One** of my friends **lives** in Madurai.*

But with *who* or *that* after the plural noun, the verb in that clause agrees with the plural noun:

> *She is one of **those who work** hard.* (Many people work hard; she is one of them.)
> *This is one of **the best films that have** been made in Tamil.*

**Exception:** with *the only one*, the verb is singular, because the clause is about that one person:

> *She is **the only one** of the students **who has** passed.*

(Modern usage varies, and many speakers use a singular verb after *one of those who*. The plural is the grammatically expected form in exams.)

### Rule 12: Some nouns end in *-s* but take a singular verb.

> ***The news is** good.* (not *~~are~~*)
> ***Mathematics is** my favourite subject.*
> ***Physics is** difficult.*
> ***Economics is** taught in Class 11.*
> ***Measles is** a disease.*

| Group | Nouns |
|---|---|
| News | news |
| Subjects | mathematics, physics, economics, politics, statistics, civics, ethics, linguistics |
| Diseases | measles, mumps, diabetes, rabies |
| Games | billiards, darts, athletics |
| Names | the United States, the Netherlands, the Philippines (as countries) |

When *politics, statistics* or *economics* mean particular opinions, figures or financial matters (not the subject), they can be plural: *His **politics are** confusing.* / *These **statistics are** not correct.*

### Rule 13: Some nouns are always plural.

**(a) Things made of two parts:** *scissors, trousers, spectacles, glasses, jeans, shorts, pliers, tongs, binoculars*.

> ***My spectacles are** broken.*
> ***These scissors are** sharp.*

With ***a pair of***, the verb is singular, because *pair* is the subject:

> ***A pair of** scissors **is** on the table.*

**(b) Nouns that look singular but are plural:** *cattle, police, people, poultry, clergy, gentry*.

> ***The police have** arrested the thief.* (not *~~has~~*)
> ***Cattle are** grazing in the field.*
> ***People are** waiting outside.*

These words have no *-s*, but they always take a plural verb.

### Rule 14: An amount of money, time, distance or weight seen as one whole takes a singular verb.

> ***Five hundred rupees is** a lot of money for a child.*
> ***Ten kilometres is** a long way to walk.*
> ***Two hours is** enough for the exam.*
> ***Fifty kilos is** too heavy to carry.*

The amount is one total, not separate items. But when the separate items are meant, the verb is plural:

> ***Ten one-rupee coins were** lying on the floor.* (ten separate coins)

### Rule 15: The title of a book, film, play or newspaper takes a singular verb.

> ***"Gitanjali" is** a collection of poems.*
> ***"Gulliver's Travels" is** a famous novel.*
> ***"The Hindu" is** read all over India.*

The title names **one** book or paper, even if the words in it are plural.

### Rule 16: After *there is / there are*, the verb agrees with the noun that follows it.

*There* is not the real subject. The real subject comes **after** the verb.

> *There **is** a temple near our house.*
> *There **are** two temples near our house.*
> *There **was** a lot of noise.*
> *There **were** many people at the wedding.*

When two or more nouns follow, the verb usually agrees with the **first** noun (the nearest one):

> *There **is** a pen and two books on the table.*
> *There **are** two books and a pen on the table.*

## Special cases

### 1. *Some, all, most, half, none, a lot of, the rest of* and fractions: look at the noun after *of*

> ***Half** of the cake **is** gone.* (uncountable / singular)
> ***Half** of the students **are** absent.* (plural)
> ***Most** of the milk **was** sour.* / ***Most** of the mangoes **were** sweet.*
> ***A lot of** time **is** wasted.* / ***A lot of** people **are** here.*
> ***Two-thirds** of the land **is** dry.* / ***Two-thirds** of the villagers **are** farmers.*

***None*** can take either: ***None** of the answers **is** correct* (formal) / ***None** of the answers **are** correct* (common). The singular is the safer choice in exams.

### 2. *The* + adjective takes a plural verb

> ***The rich are** not always happy.*
> ***The poor need** help.*

### 3. Uncountable nouns take a singular verb

> ***The furniture is** new.* / ***This information is** useful.* / ***My luggage is** heavy.* / ***Your advice was** helpful.*

### 4. *Many a* + singular noun takes a singular verb

> ***Many a** student **has** made this mistake.*

### 5. *More than one* + singular noun takes a singular verb

> ***More than one** student **was** late.*

### 6. The relative pronoun agrees with the noun or pronoun before it

> *It is **I who am** responsible.* (*who* = *I* → *am*)
> *It is **you who are** wrong.*
> *He is the **boy who has** won.*

### 7. Two infinitives or gerunds joined by *and*

> ***Reading and writing are** important skills.* (two activities)
> ***Swimming is** good exercise.* (one gerund → singular)

### 8. Nationalities

*The English, the French, the Japanese* (= the people) take a plural verb: ***The Japanese are** hard-working.* But *Japanese* (the language) is singular: ***Japanese is** difficult to learn.*

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The box of mangoes ~~are~~ heavy.* | *The box of mangoes **is** heavy.* | The subject is *box*, not *mangoes*. |
| *Bread and butter ~~are~~ my breakfast.* | *Bread and butter **is** my breakfast.* | One dish → singular. |
| *Neither Ravi nor his friends ~~was~~ there.* | *Neither Ravi nor his friends **were** there.* | Agree with the nearer subject (*friends*). |
| *The teacher, along with the students, ~~are~~ here.* | *The teacher, along with the students, **is** here.* | *Along with* does not join subjects. The subject is *the teacher*. |
| *Each of the girls ~~have~~ a book.* | *Each of the girls **has** a book.* | *Each* takes a singular verb. |
| *The news ~~are~~ true.* | *The news **is** true.* | *News* is singular. |
| *Mathematics ~~are~~ easy for her.* | *Mathematics **is** easy for her.* | A subject name ending in *-ics* is singular. |
| *The police ~~has~~ come.* | *The police **have** come.* | *Police* is always plural. |
| *The number of cars ~~are~~ increasing.* | *The number of cars **is** increasing.* | *The number* (one figure) → singular. |
| *A number of students ~~was~~ absent.* | *A number of students **were** absent.* | *A number of* = many → plural. |
| *One of my friends ~~live~~ in Salem.* | *One of my friends **lives** in Salem.* | The subject is *one* → singular. |
| *There ~~is~~ many reasons.* | *There **are** many reasons.* | Agree with *reasons*, the noun after *there are*. |
| *Five hundred rupees ~~are~~ too much.* | *Five hundred rupees **is** too much.* | An amount as one whole → singular. |
| *My trousers ~~is~~ torn.* | *My trousers **are** torn.* | *Trousers* is always plural. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Choose the correct verb** from two options: *(is / are), (has / have), (was / were)*.
- **Fill in the blanks** with *is / are* or *has / have*.
- **Spot the error** and correct it.

### Steps

1. **Find the verb** (the blank or the underlined word).
2. **Find the real subject.**
   - Cover any *of...* phrase or *along with / as well as...* phrase.
   - After *there is / there are*, look **after** the verb.
3. **Check the joining word** if there are two subjects:
   - *and* → plural (unless one idea or one person).
   - *or, nor, either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also* → agree with the nearer subject.
   - *with, along with, as well as* → agree with the first subject.
4. **Check the special words**:
   - *each, every, everyone, nobody, either, neither* → singular.
   - *the number of* → singular; *a number of* → plural.
   - *one of the* + plural → singular.
   - *news, mathematics, measles* → singular. *scissors, police, cattle* → plural.
   - amount of money / time / distance → singular. Title → singular.
   - collective noun → singular (one unit) or plural (members acting separately).
5. **Choose the verb** and read the sentence again.

### Worked example 1

> *The quality of the bananas (is / are) not good.*

1. Verb: *is / are*.
2. Real subject: cover *of the bananas* → *The quality*.
3. *Quality* is singular.

**Answer:** *The quality of the bananas **is** not good.*

### Worked example 2

> *Neither the principal nor the teachers (was / were) informed.*

1. Verb: *was / were*.
2. Two subjects joined by *neither ... nor*.
3. Agree with the nearer subject: *the teachers* (plural).

**Answer:** *Neither the principal nor the teachers **were** informed.*

### Worked example 3

> *Arun, as well as his brothers, (has / have) joined the NCC.*

1. Verb: *has / have*.
2. *As well as* does not join subjects. Cover *as well as his brothers*.
3. Real subject: *Arun* (singular).

**Answer:** *Arun, as well as his brothers, **has** joined the NCC.*

### Worked example 4

> *Spot the error: A number of trees has been cut down.*

1. Verb: *has been cut*.
2. Subject: *A number of trees*.
3. *A number of* = many → plural.

**Answer:** *A number of trees **have** been cut down.*

### Worked example 5

> *The jury (is / are) divided in their opinions.*

1. Verb: *is / are*.
2. Subject: *the jury* (a collective noun).
3. Clues: *divided*, *their opinions* → the members are acting separately.

**Answer:** *The jury **are** divided in their opinions.*

## Quick check

1. *The furniture in these rooms (is / are) old.*
2. *Either my parents or my uncle (is / are) coming to the meeting.*
3. *Physics (is / are) my favourite subject.*
4. *The cattle (is / are) grazing near the river.*
5. *Twenty kilometres (is / are) too far to walk.*

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. ***is.*** The subject is *furniture* (uncountable), not *rooms*.
2. ***is.*** *Either ... or*: agree with the nearer subject, *my uncle* (singular).
3. ***is.*** *Physics* is the name of a subject. It looks plural, but it is singular.
4. ***are.*** *Cattle* is always plural.
5. ***is.*** A distance seen as one whole takes a singular verb.

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **Find the real subject.** Cover *of...* phrases and *along with / as well as...* phrases.
- *and* → plural, except one idea or one person (*bread and butter **is***, *the secretary and treasurer **is***).
- *either / neither ... or / nor, not only ... but also* → agree with the **nearer** subject.
- *each, every, everyone, nobody, either, neither, one of the...* → singular.
- *The number of* → singular. *A number of* → plural.
- *news, mathematics, measles*, amounts, titles → singular. *scissors, trousers, police, cattle, people* → plural.
- Collective nouns: singular for one unit, plural for members acting separately.
- *There is / are*: agree with the noun after the verb.
:::
