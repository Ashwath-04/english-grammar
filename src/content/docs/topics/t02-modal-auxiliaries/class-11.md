## What you will learn

- What a **modal auxiliary** is, and the four features that make modals different from other verbs
- Which modal to use for each **function** (meaning):
  - ability, permission, possibility
  - obligation, prohibition, lack of necessity
  - advice
  - requests, offers and suggestions
  - willingness and determination
  - past habits
- How to answer "Fill in with a suitable modal", "Identify the function" and "Rewrite using a modal"

## Why this matters

Modals let us say more than plain facts. Compare *You go* with *You **must** go*, *You **may** go*, *You **should** go* and *You **can** go*. The main verb is the same, but each modal changes the meaning completely.

In the exam you will be asked to fill in modals, name their function, and rewrite sentences with them. Modals also appear in [reported speech](/topics/t12-reported-speech/class-11/) (*can → could*) and the [passive voice](/topics/t11-active-passive/class-12/) (*can be done*).

## The idea in simple words

Imagine Ravi wants to go to a cricket match. Look at what different people might say:

> *Ravi **can** play cricket very well.* (he has the skill)
> *"You **may** go to the match," said his father.* (permission)
> *It **might** rain during the match.* (possibility)
> *He **must** finish his homework first.* (it is necessary)
> *He **should** take an umbrella.* (good advice)
> ***Will** you buy me a ticket?* (request)

The words in bold are **modal auxiliaries**. They add an attitude or a meaning to the main verb: skill, permission, chance, duty, advice, and so on.

## Grammar words you need

**Modal auxiliary (modal verb):** a helping verb that adds meaning like ability, permission or duty. The main modals are *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must* and *ought to*.

**Semi-modal:** an expression that works like a modal but is formed differently: *have to, need to, be able to, had better, used to*.

**Main verb:** the verb that carries the action. In *She can **sing***, the main verb is *sing*.

**V1 (bare infinitive):** the base form of a verb without *to*: *go, eat, sing*. See [Verb forms](/foundation/f2-verb-forms/).

**Function:** the job or meaning of the modal in a sentence. For example, *can* in *I can swim* has the function of **ability**.

## The rules

### Rule 1: A modal is always followed by V1.

> *She **can sing**.* (not *~~can sings~~* or *~~can to sing~~*)
> *You **must wear** a helmet.*
> *It **may rain** today.*

The only exception is ***ought to***, which has *to* built into it: *You **ought to** respect your elders.*

### Rule 2: A modal never changes for the subject.

There is no *-s* with *he, she* or *it*.

> *I can / you can / **she can** / they can* (not *~~she cans~~*)
> *He **must** go.* (not *~~He musts go~~*)

### Rule 3: A modal makes its own negative and question, without *do*.

| Positive | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|
| *He can swim.* | *He **cannot** (can't) swim.* | ***Can** he swim?* |
| *You should go.* | *You **should not** (shouldn't) go.* | ***Should** I go?* |
| *We must leave.* | *We **must not** (mustn't) leave.* | ***Must** we leave?* |
| *She will come.* | *She **will not** (won't) come.* | ***Will** she come?* |

Note: *cannot* is written as **one word**.

### Rule 4: A modal has no *-ing* form, no *to* form and no V3. Use a semi-modal instead.

We cannot say *~~to can~~*, *~~canning~~* or *~~have musted~~*. When we need these forms, we use a semi-modal:

| Instead of | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *can* | *be able to* | *I **will be able to** come tomorrow.* (not *~~will can~~*) |
| *must* | *have to* | *She **has had to** wait for an hour.* |
| *can* (after another verb) | *be able to* | *I hope **to be able to** help you.* |

### Rule 5: Use *can*, *could* and *be able to* for ability.

| Time | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present | *can* / *am, is, are able to* | *Kavya **can** speak three languages.* |
| Past (general ability) | *could* / *was, were able to* | *When I was five, I **could** swim.* |
| Future | *will be able to* | *After the course, you **will be able to** type fast.* |

**Could or was able to?** For a **general** past ability, both are fine. For **one particular** success in the past, use *was able to* (or *managed to*), not *could*:

> *The fire spread quickly, but everyone **was able to** escape.* (not *~~could escape~~*)

In the **negative**, *couldn't* is fine for both: *I **couldn't** open the door.*

### Rule 6: Use *may*, *can* and *could* for permission.

| Modal | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *may* | formal permission (asking or giving) | ***May** I come in, sir?* / *You **may** leave now.* |
| *can* | informal permission | ***Can** I borrow your pen?* / *You **can** sit here.* |
| *could* | polite request for permission | ***Could** I use your phone, please?* |

*Could* is used to **ask** for permission politely. To **give** permission, use *can* or *may*: *Yes, you **can**.* (not *~~Yes, you could~~*)

Refusing permission: *No, you **may not**.* / *No, you **can't**.*

### Rule 7: Use *may*, *might* and *could* for possibility.

> *Take an umbrella. It **may** rain.* (possible)
> *Arun **might** come to the party, but I'm not sure.* (less sure)
> *That **could** be the postman at the door.* (possible)

*Might* usually shows a little **less** certainty than *may*, but in everyday use they are very close.

Negative possibility: ***may not / might not***. *She **may not** know the answer.* (= perhaps she doesn't know)

Be careful: *can't* is not the negative of *may* here. *She **can't** know the answer* means "I'm sure she doesn't know". See [Modal auxiliaries (Class 12)](/topics/t02-modal-auxiliaries/class-12/).

### Rule 8: Use *must*, *have to* and *need to* for obligation and necessity.

> *Students **must** wear the school uniform.* (a rule, or the speaker's strong feeling)
> *I **have to** catch the 7 o'clock bus.* (a need from outside: the bus timetable)
> *You **need to** fill in this form.* (it is necessary)

| Time | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present | *must* / *have to, has to* | *He **has to** work on Sundays.* |
| Past | *had to* (*must* has no past form) | *We **had to** walk home yesterday.* |
| Future | *will have to* | *You **will have to** wait.* |

### Rule 9: Use *must not* and *cannot* for prohibition (when something is not allowed).

> *You **must not** use your phone in the exam hall.*
> *Visitors **cannot** park here.*
> *You **mustn't** cross the road when the signal is red.*

*Must not* = it is **wrong** or **not allowed** to do it.

### Rule 10: Use *need not* and *don't have to* when something is not necessary.

> *You **need not** come tomorrow. It's a holiday.*
> *You **don't have to** bring lunch. We will give you food.*
> *She **doesn't need to** buy a new uniform.*

This is very different from *must not*:

> *You **must not** go.* (It is not allowed. Don't go.)
> *You **need not** go.* (It is not necessary. You can choose.)

**Form:** *need not* + V1 (no *to*). *don't need to* + V1 (with *to*). *~~You need not to worry~~* is wrong.

### Rule 11: Use *should*, *ought to* and *had better* for advice.

> *You **should** drink more water.*
> *You **ought to** apologise to her.*
> *You **had better** leave now, or you'll miss the train.*

| Modal | Strength | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *should* | normal advice | the most common |
| *ought to* | normal advice, often a moral duty | a little more formal; negative *ought not to* |
| *had better* ('d better) | strong advice, with a warning | for one particular situation, not general advice |

*Had better* refers to the present or future, even though it uses *had*. It is followed by V1: *You **had better go*** (not *~~had better to go~~*). Negative: *You **had better not** go.*

### Rule 12: Use *will, would, can* and *could* for requests; *shall* and *can* for offers; *shall* and *let's* for suggestions.

**Requests** (asking someone to do something):

> ***Can** you help me?* (informal)
> ***Will** you close the door, please?*
> ***Could** you lend me your notes?* (polite)
> ***Would** you please explain this again?* (very polite)

**Offers** (offering to do something for someone):

> ***Shall** I carry your bag?*
> ***Can** I get you a glass of water?*
> *I **will** drop you at the station.*

**Suggestions:**

> ***Shall** we go for a walk?*
> ***Let's** have dinner at the hotel.*
> *We **could** watch a film tonight.*

### Rule 13: Use *will* and *shall* for willingness, promises and determination.

**Willingness** (being ready to do something):

> *I **will** help you with your project.* (I am willing.)
> *He **won't** listen to anyone.* (He refuses.)

**Promise and determination:**

> *I **will** pass this exam, whatever happens!* (determination)
> *You **shall** have your money tomorrow.* (a promise from the speaker: formal)
> *They **shall not** pass!* (strong determination of the speaker: formal)

*Shall* with *you, he, she, they* shows the **speaker's** promise, command or determination. It is formal and found in rules and older English: *Candidates **shall** write only in blue or black ink.*

### Rule 14: Use *would* and *used to* for past habits.

> *When we were children, we **used to** play in the river every summer.*
> *Every evening, my grandfather **would** tell us stories.*

| Form | Past repeated actions | Past states |
|---|---|---|
| *used to* + V1 | ✔ *I **used to** walk to school.* | ✔ *I **used to** live in Salem.* |
| *would* + V1 | ✔ *I **would** walk to school.* | ✘ *~~I would live in Salem.~~* |

**Questions and negatives of *used to*:** *Did you **use to** play cricket?* / *I didn't **use to** like vegetables.* (no *-d* after *did*)

## Special cases

### 1. *Cannot* is one word

> *I **cannot** swim.* ✔ (not *~~can not~~* in normal use)

*Can not* is used only in rare cases where *not* belongs to the next words: *You can **not only** read but also write.*

### 2. *May not* and *might not*: two meanings

> *You **may not** use the library today.* (permission refused)
> *He **may not** come today.* (possibility: perhaps he won't come)

The context tells you which meaning it is.

### 3. *Be able to* for future ability

*Can* can refer to the future when we decide or arrange something now: *I **can** see you tomorrow.* But for a future ability that does not exist now, use *will be able to*:

> *After six months of practice, you **will be able to** play the guitar.*

### 4. *Must* or *have to*

> *I **must** study harder.* (I feel it is necessary.)
> *I **have to** wear a tie at school.* (It is a school rule. Someone else decided.)

In British English, this difference is common. In American English, *have to* is used for both. Both are accepted in exams. The Class 12 lesson covers this in detail.

### 5. *Need* and *dare* as ordinary verbs and as modals

*Need* can be an ordinary verb (with *to*, *does*, *-s*) or a modal (no *to*, no *-s*), mostly in negatives and questions:

> *He **needs to** rest.* (ordinary verb)
> *He **need not** worry.* (modal) / ***Need** I come?* (modal)

The full lesson is in [Modal auxiliaries (Class 12)](/topics/t02-modal-auxiliaries/class-12/).

### 6. *Would* in polite expressions

> ***Would you like** some tea?* (offer)
> ***I would like** to see the manager.* (polite wish)
> ***Would you mind** opening the window?* (polite request: *mind* + V4)

### 7. *Could* as the past of *can* and as a polite form

*Could* has two different uses:

> *When she was young, she **could** dance very well.* (past ability)
> ***Could** you pass the salt?* (polite request, present)

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *She can ~~sings~~.* | *She can **sing**.* | A modal is followed by V1. |
| *You must ~~to~~ go.* | *You must **go**.* | No *to* after modals (except *ought to*). |
| *He ~~can able to~~ do it.* | *He **can** do it.* / *He **is able to** do it.* | Use either *can* or *be able to*, not both. |
| *I ~~will can~~ come.* | *I **will be able to** come.* | Two modals cannot go together. Use *be able to*. |
| *You need not ~~to~~ worry.* | *You **need not** worry.* / *You **don't need to** worry.* | Modal *need not* takes V1 without *to*. |
| *You had better ~~to~~ see a doctor.* | *You had better **see** a doctor.* | *Had better* + V1. |
| *Did you ~~used to~~ live here?* | *Did you **use to** live here?* | After *did*, use *use to* (no *-d*). |
| *You ~~must not~~ come tomorrow. It's a holiday.* | *You **need not** come tomorrow. It's a holiday.* | It is not necessary, not forbidden. Use *need not*. |
| *Yesterday I ~~must~~ go to the bank.* | *Yesterday I **had to** go to the bank.* | *Must* has no past form. Use *had to*. |
| *He ~~musts~~ obey the rules.* | *He **must** obey the rules.* | Modals never take *-s*. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Fill in with a suitable modal** (sometimes from a box of choices).
- **Identify the function** of the modal in a sentence (ability, permission, advice...).
- **Rewrite the sentence using a modal**: *It is possible that he is ill* → *He **may** be ill.*

### Steps for "Fill in with a suitable modal"

1. **Read the whole sentence**, including any second sentence that explains it.
2. **Decide the meaning**: is it about skill, permission, chance, duty, a rule, advice, a request, a promise or a past habit?
3. **Decide the time**: present, past or future? (*must → had to* in the past; *can → could* or *was able to*.)
4. **Decide the strength**: very sure or not sure? strong duty or soft advice? polite or informal?
5. **Check the form**: V1 after the modal; *ought to* keeps its *to*.

### Steps for "Rewrite using a modal"

1. Find the key phrase: *It is possible that...*, *It is necessary...*, *He is able to...*, *It is not allowed...*, *I advise you to...*
2. Choose the modal that has that meaning (see the table below).
3. Put the modal before the main verb in V1. Remove the extra words.

| Phrase | Modal |
|---|---|
| is able to / knows how to | can |
| was able to (general past ability) | could |
| it is possible that / perhaps | may / might |
| it is necessary / it is compulsory | must / have to |
| it is not allowed / it is forbidden | must not |
| it is not necessary | need not / don't have to |
| it is advisable / I advise you to | should / ought to |
| please allow me to | may I / could I |
| used to do (repeatedly) | would |

### Worked example 1

> *______ I use your phone? Mine is not working.*

1. Meaning: asking for **permission**.
2. Time: now.
3. Strength: polite (to someone else's phone).
4. Choices: *May I* (formal), *Could I* (polite), *Can I* (informal). All are correct.

**Answer:** ***May*** (or ***Could*** / ***Can***) *I use your phone?*

### Worked example 2

> *You ______ shout in the hospital. Patients are resting.*

1. Meaning: something that is **not allowed**.
2. Time: present.
3. Strength: strong.
4. Prohibition → *must not*.

**Answer:** *You **must not** shout in the hospital.* (*should not* is also acceptable as strong advice, but *must not* fits a rule best.)

### Worked example 3

> *Rewrite using a modal: It is possible that Meena is at the library.*

1. Key phrase: *It is possible that* → possibility.
2. Modal: *may* (or *might*).
3. Put it before V1: *is* → *be*.

**Answer:** *Meena **may be** at the library.* (*might be* and *could be* are also correct.)

### Worked example 4

> *Identify the function: When I was young, I **would** climb trees every evening.*

1. The sentence is about the past (*When I was young*).
2. The action happened again and again (*every evening*).
3. *Would* + V1 for a repeated past action → past habit.

**Answer:** **Past habit.**

### Worked example 5

> *Rewrite using a modal: It is not necessary for you to bring your laptop.*

1. Key phrase: *not necessary* → lack of necessity.
2. Modal: *need not*.
3. *Need not* + V1.

**Answer:** *You **need not** bring your laptop.* (*You **don't have to** bring your laptop* is also correct.)

## Quick check

1. *Divya ______ play the veena when she was only six.* (ability, past)
2. *You ______ eat so many sweets. It is bad for your teeth.* (advice)
3. *______ I carry your bag for you?* (offer)
4. Identify the function: *You **needn't** wait for me.*
5. Correct the error: *He can to speak Hindi.*

1. ***could.*** General ability in the past → *could* (or *was able to*).
2. ***shouldn't*** (or ***ought not to***). Advice not to do something.

4. **Lack of necessity.** *Needn't* means it is not necessary; you can choose.
5. ***He can speak Hindi.*** After a modal, use V1 without *to*.

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- Modal + **V1**. No *-s*, no *to* (except *ought to*), no *-ing*, no *do* in questions.
- Ability: *can, could, be able to*. Permission: *may, can, could*. Possibility: *may, might, could*.
- Obligation: *must, have to* (past: *had to*). Prohibition: *must not*. No need: *need not, don't have to*.
- Advice: *should, ought to, had better*.
- Requests: *can, will, could, would*. Offers: *shall I, can I*. Suggestions: *shall we, let's*.
- Past habit: *used to* (actions and states), *would* (actions only).
:::
