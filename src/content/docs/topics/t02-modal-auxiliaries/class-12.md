- that a modal is followed by V1, with no *-s* and no *to* (except *ought to*)
- the main functions: ability, permission, possibility, obligation, prohibition, advice, requests, past habit
- that *must* has no past form, so we use *had to*
- the difference between *must not* (not allowed) and *need not* (not necessary)

## What you will learn

- How to talk about the **past** with modal + *have* + V3: *must have, can't have, might have, should have, could have, needn't have*
- **Deduction**: how sure you are about something (*He must be tired / He might be tired / He can't be tired*)
- Fine differences: *must* vs *have to*; *mustn't* vs *needn't*; *may* vs *might*; *shall* vs *will*
- *Dare* and *need* as modals and as ordinary verbs
- Semi-modals: *used to*, *ought to*, *be supposed to*

## Why this matters

In Class 11 you used modals to talk about the present and future. Real life also needs the past: *You **should have told** me! She **must have forgotten**. They **needn't have waited**.*

These forms come up in every exam: fill in the blanks, identify the function, and rewrite sentences like *I'm sure he has left* → *He **must have left***. They are also tested in error correction.

## The idea in simple words

Imagine you come home and find the floor wet and the window open.

> *It **must have rained**.* (I'm almost sure. The evidence is clear.)
> *Amma **might have opened** the window.* (Perhaps. I'm not sure.)
> *It **can't have been** the cat. The cat is at Grandma's house.* (I'm sure it wasn't.)
> *Someone **should have closed** the window!* (It was the right thing to do, but no one did it.)

All four are about the **past**. Each one uses **modal + *have* + V3**. The modal shows how sure you are, or what you think about the action.

## Grammar words you need

**Modal perfect:** the pattern modal + *have* + V3: *must have gone, should have told*. It talks about the past.

**Deduction:** a conclusion we reach from evidence. *The lights are off. They **must be** asleep.*

**Certainty:** how sure the speaker is. *Must* = almost sure (yes). *Might* = not sure. *Can't* = almost sure (no).

**Criticism / regret:** saying that something in the past was wrong, or that you are sorry about it. *You **should have studied**.*

**Semi-modal:** an expression that works like a modal but has a different form: *have to, used to, ought to, be supposed to, be able to*.

**Speaker's authority:** the speaker personally decides or feels the need. *I **must** stop eating sweets.*

## The rules

### Rule 1: Modal + *have* + V3 talks about the past.

| Present / future | Past |
|---|---|
| *He **must be** at home.* | *He **must have been** at home.* |
| *She **may know** the answer.* | *She **may have known** the answer.* |
| *You **should tell** him.* | *You **should have told** him.* |

*Have* never changes to *has* or *had* after a modal: *She must **have** gone* (not *~~must has gone~~*).

### Rule 2: Use *must have* + V3 when you are almost sure something happened.

> *The road is wet. It **must have rained** last night.*
> *Kavya is smiling. She **must have passed** the exam.*
> *You **must have been** tired after the long journey.*

*Must have* here is **deduction**, not obligation. There is no idea of duty. For past obligation, use *had to*: *I **had to** wait for an hour.*

### Rule 3: Use *can't have* or *couldn't have* + V3 when you are almost sure something did not happen.

> *Ravi **can't have stolen** the money. He was in Coimbatore that day.*
> *She **couldn't have seen** me. It was too dark.*

The opposite of *must have* (sure: yes) is *can't have* (sure: no). We do **not** use *mustn't have* for this in British English.

### Rule 4: Use *may have*, *might have* or *could have* + V3 when perhaps something happened.

> *Arun is late. He **may have missed** the bus.*
> *I can't find my keys. I **might have left** them in the office.*
> *The letter **could have been** lost in the post.*

All three mean "perhaps it happened; I'm not sure".

### Rule 5: Use *could have* or *might have* + V3 for something that was possible but did not happen.

> *You **could have been** killed! You were lucky.* (It was possible, but you weren't.)
> *Why did you walk? You **could have taken** an auto.* (You were able to, but you didn't.)
> *I **could have won** the race, but I fell.*

### Rule 6: Use *should have* or *ought to have* + V3 for the right action that was not done.

> *You **should have told** me earlier.* (You didn't tell me. That was wrong.)
> *I **ought to have studied** harder.* (I didn't. I regret it.)

The negative is the opposite: something was **done**, but it was wrong.

> *You **shouldn't have shouted** at her.* (You did shout. That was wrong.)
> *We **oughtn't to have** spent so much.*

### Rule 7: Use *needn't have* + V3 when something was done but was not necessary.

> *You **needn't have bought** milk. We already had some.* (You bought it. It was not necessary.)
> *I **needn't have hurried**. The train was late.*

Compare with ***didn't need to*** + V1, which usually means it was not necessary, **so it was not done**:

> *I **didn't need to** hurry, so I walked slowly.* (I did not hurry.)
> *I **needn't have hurried**.* (I did hurry, and it was a waste.)

*Didn't need to* can also be used when the action was done, but *needn't have* + V3 **always** means it was done.

### Rule 8: Use *would have* + V3 for an imaginary past result.

> *I **would have helped** you, but you didn't ask.*
> *If she had left earlier, she **would have caught** the train.*

This is the result part of a Type 3 conditional. See [Conditional clauses (Class 11)](/topics/t13-conditionals/class-11/).

### Rule 9: For deduction about the present, use *must* (sure: yes), *may / might / could* (perhaps), and *can't* (sure: no).

| How sure? | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|
| Almost sure: yes | *He **must be** tired.* | *He **must have been** tired.* |
| Perhaps | *He **may / might / could be** tired.* | *He **may / might / could have been** tired.* |
| Almost sure: no | *He **can't / couldn't be** tired.* | *He **can't / couldn't have been** tired.* |

> *Divya has been studying for ten hours. She **must be** exhausted.*
> *That **can't be** Meena. She is in Madurai today.*
> *The shop **might be** closed. It's Sunday.*

**Continuous form:** *modal + be + V4* for something going on now. *Don't call him now. He **must be sleeping**.*

**Note:** American English sometimes uses *must not* for negative deduction (*He must not be home*). In British English and in exams, use *can't*.

### Rule 10: *Must* shows the speaker's own feeling or rule. *Have to* shows a need from outside.

> *I **must** stop eating junk food.* (I have decided.)
> *I **have to** wear a uniform at school.* (It is the school's rule.)
> *You **must** come to my wedding!* (The speaker strongly wants it.)
> *We **have to** pay the electricity bill by Monday.* (The electricity board says so.)

| | *must* | *have to* |
|---|---|---|
| Where the need comes from | the speaker | outside: a rule, a law, a situation |
| Past | (no form) *had to* | *had to* |
| Future | *must* (for a decision now) / *will have to* | *will have to* |
| Question | *Must I go?* (formal) | *Do I have to go?* (common) |

In everyday speech, especially in American English, *have to* is used for both. If an exam gives only one choice, either is usually accepted.

### Rule 11: *Mustn't* means "don't do it". *Needn't* and *don't have to* mean "it is not necessary".

This is the most important difference among the modals.

| | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *must not (mustn't)* | It is **not allowed** / it is wrong. | *You **mustn't** tell anyone. It's a secret.* |
| *need not (needn't)* | It is **not necessary**. You can choose. | *You **needn't** come early. We start at ten.* |
| *don't have to* | It is **not necessary**. | *You **don't have to** wear a tie on Saturdays.* |

> *You mustn't eat it.* (Don't eat it.)
> *You needn't eat it.* (You can eat it if you want, but it is not necessary.)

Notice: *must* and *have to* mean almost the same, but *mustn't* and *don't have to* mean very different things.

### Rule 12: *Might* is a little less sure than *may*. *Might* is also the past of *may* in reported speech.

> *It **may** rain.* (possible)
> *It **might** rain.* (possible, a little less sure)

In everyday English the difference is small, and both are accepted in most exams.

*Might* must be used:

- in reported speech after a past reporting verb: *She said, "I **may** come."* → *She said that she **might** come.*
- for imaginary situations and Type 2 conditionals: *If you asked her, she **might** agree.*
- for polite suggestions: *You **might** try the new hotel.*

### Rule 13: Use *will* for the simple future with every subject. Use *shall* with *I* and *we* in offers, suggestions and formal English.

**Modern usage (follow this in exams):**

| Use | *will* | *shall* |
|---|---|---|
| Simple future | all persons: *I / you / he **will** go.* | *I / we **shall** go* (formal British) |
| Offers and suggestions | — | ***Shall** I help? **Shall** we start?* |
| Promises, threats, rules | *I **will** pay you back.* | *You **shall** have your reward.* (formal) / *Students **shall** not use phones.* (rules) |

**Traditional rule** (found in older grammar books such as Wren & Martin): for the simple future, use *shall* with *I / we* and *will* with *you / he / she / they*. To show **determination, a promise or a command**, swap them: *I **will** go* (I am determined); *You **shall** go* (I order or promise it). This rule is rarely followed in modern English, but teachers may meet it in older exercises.

### Rule 14: *Dare* and *need* can be modals (no *-s*, no *to*) in negatives and questions.

| | As a modal | As an ordinary verb |
|---|---|---|
| *need* | *He **need not** come.* / ***Need** I come?* | *He **needs to** come.* / *Does he **need to** come?* / *He **doesn't need to** come.* |
| *dare* | *He **dare not** speak.* / ***Dare** she ask?* / ***How dare** you!* | *He **doesn't dare to** speak.* / *Does she **dare to** ask?* |

As modals, *need* and *dare* are used mostly in **negatives and questions**. In positive sentences, they are ordinary verbs: *He **needs** to rest.* (not *~~He need rest~~*)

*How dare you!* is a fixed expression that shows anger.

### Rule 15: The semi-modals *used to*, *ought to* and *be supposed to* have their own forms.

**Used to** + V1: a past habit or state that is no longer true.

> *I **used to** play kabaddi.* / *Did you **use to** play?* / *I didn't **use to** like coffee.*

**Ought to** + V1: duty or advice (like *should*).

> *You **ought to** visit your grandparents.* / *You **ought not to** waste food.* / *You **ought to have** called.*

**Be supposed to** + V1: what is expected because of a rule, an arrangement or what people say.

> *We **are supposed to** be in class by 8.45.* (the rule)
> *The train **was supposed to** arrive at 6, but it came at 7.* (the plan, which did not happen)
> *This film **is supposed to** be very good.* (people say so)

*Was / were supposed to* often means the expected thing **did not happen**.

## Special cases

### 1. *Used to* + V1 or *be used to* + V4

These look alike but are very different.

> *I **used to live** in a village.* (a past state, not true now)
> *I **am used to living** in a village.* (I am accustomed to it. It is not strange to me.)
> *She **got used to waking** up early.* (She became accustomed to it.)

*Be / get used to* is followed by a noun or V4 (*-ing*), because *to* here is a preposition.

### 2. *Must have* + V3 never means past obligation

> *~~Yesterday I must have gone to the bank~~* (to mean "it was necessary") ✘
> *Yesterday I **had to go** to the bank.* ✔

*Must have gone* means "I'm sure someone went".

### 3. *Could have* and *was able to*

> *I **was able to** finish the work.* (I finished it.)
> *I **could have finished** the work.* (I had the chance, but I didn't finish it.)

### 4. *Should* after some verbs and adjectives

In British English, *should* is used after *suggest, recommend, insist, important, necessary* to show what is wanted:

> *The doctor suggested that he **should rest**.*
> *It is important that every student **should attend**.*

American English often drops *should* and uses V1: *The doctor suggested that he **rest***. Both are correct.

### 5. Modals in the passive

> *The work **must be done** today.* / *The letter **should have been posted** yesterday.*

See [Active and passive voice (Class 12)](/topics/t11-active-passive/class-12/).

### 6. *Would rather* and *would sooner*

> *I **would rather** stay at home than go out.* (preference: + V1 without *to*)
> *I'd rather not say.*

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *He must ~~has gone~~.* | *He must **have gone**.* | After a modal, always *have*, never *has* or *had*. |
| *You should ~~have came~~ earlier.* | *You should **have come** earlier.* | Modal + *have* + **V3**. *Come – came – come*. |
| *She ~~mustn't have~~ seen me. It was too dark.* | *She **can't have** seen me.* | Negative deduction about the past → *can't have* / *couldn't have*. |
| *I ~~must have~~ go to the bank yesterday (it was necessary).* | *I **had to** go to the bank yesterday.* | Past obligation is *had to*. *Must have* is deduction. |
| *You ~~mustn't~~ bring food. We will provide it.* | *You **needn't** / **don't have to** bring food.* | It is not necessary, not forbidden. |
| *I ~~needn't have gone~~, so I stayed at home.* | *I **didn't need to** go, so I stayed at home.* | *Needn't have* + V3 means the action **was done**. Here I did not go. |
| *He ~~need~~ rest.* | *He **needs to** rest.* | In positive sentences, *need* is an ordinary verb: *needs to*. |
| *I am used to ~~wake~~ up early.* | *I am used to **waking** up early.* | *Be used to* + V4. |
| *They ~~are supposed~~ arrive at 6.* | *They are supposed **to** arrive at 6.* | *Be supposed* **to** + V1. |
| *He ~~can't be~~ come yesterday.* | *He **can't have come** yesterday.* | For the past, use modal + *have* + V3. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Fill in with a suitable modal** (present or past form).
- **Identify the function**: deduction, criticism, unnecessary past action, unfulfilled possibility...
- **Rewrite using a modal**:
  - *I'm sure he has left.* → *He **must have left**.*
  - *Perhaps she missed the bus.* → *She **may have missed** the bus.*
  - *It was not necessary for you to buy it, but you did.* → *You **needn't have bought** it.*

### Steps for "Rewrite using a modal"

1. **Find the time.** Past → modal + *have* + V3. Present → modal + V1 (or *be* + V4).
2. **Find the meaning** from the key phrase:

| Key phrase | Present | Past |
|---|---|---|
| *I'm sure that... / I'm certain that...* | must + V1 | must have + V3 |
| *I'm sure that... not* / *It is impossible that...* | can't + V1 | can't have + V3 |
| *Perhaps... / It is possible that...* | may / might + V1 | may / might have + V3 |
| *It was a good idea, but you didn't...* / *It was wrong not to...* | — | should have + V3 |
| *It was wrong to...* (you did it) | — | shouldn't have + V3 |
| *It was not necessary, but you did...* | — | needn't have + V3 |
| *It was possible, but you didn't...* | — | could have + V3 |

3. **Build the sentence**: subject + modal + (*have* + V3 / V1) + the rest.
4. **Check** that *have* is not *has*, and that the verb is V3.

### Worked example 1

> *Rewrite using a modal: I'm sure Karthik has forgotten my birthday.*

1. Time: past (*has forgotten*).
2. Meaning: *I'm sure* → *must*.
3. Past → *must have* + V3: *must have forgotten*.

**Answer:** *Karthik **must have forgotten** my birthday.*

### Worked example 2

> *Rewrite using a modal: It was not necessary for you to cook, but you did.*

1. Time: past.
2. Meaning: not necessary, but done → *needn't have*.
3. *needn't have* + V3: *needn't have cooked*.

**Answer:** *You **needn't have cooked**.*

### Worked example 3

> *Fill in: Priya scored 98 in maths. She ______ (study) very hard.*

1. Time: the studying was in the past → modal + *have* + V3.
2. Meaning: a strong conclusion from evidence (98 marks) → *must*.

**Answer:** *She **must have studied** very hard.*

### Worked example 4

> *Rewrite using a modal: It was wrong of you to leave the door unlocked.*

1. Time: past.
2. Meaning: you did it, and it was wrong → *shouldn't have*.
3. *shouldn't have* + V3: *shouldn't have left*.

**Answer:** *You **shouldn't have left** the door unlocked.*

### Worked example 5

> *Identify the function: You **could have asked** me for help.*

1. Form: *could have* + V3 → past.
2. Meaning: it was possible to ask, but you didn't. There is a mild complaint.

**Answer:** **Unfulfilled past possibility** (a past chance that was not taken; mild criticism).

## Quick check

1. *The ground is dry. It ______ rained last night.* (can't have / must have)
2. *You ______ come with me. I can go alone.* (mustn't / needn't)
3. Rewrite: *Perhaps Meena took the wrong bus.*
4. *I'm sorry. I ______ shouted at you.* (shouldn't have / mustn't have)
5. Correct the error: *They are used to play cricket in the evening.*

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. ***can't have.*** If it had rained, the ground would be wet. The speaker is sure it did **not** rain.
2. ***needn't.*** It is not necessary for you to come; it is not forbidden. *Mustn't* would mean "you are not allowed to come".
3. ***Meena may have taken the wrong bus.*** (or *might have taken*.) *Perhaps* + past → *may / might have* + V3.
4. ***shouldn't have.*** I did shout, and it was wrong. *Mustn't have* is not used this way.
5. ***They used to play cricket in the evening*** (a past habit) or ***They are used to playing cricket in the evening*** (it is normal for them). *Be used to* needs V4.

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- **Past** with modals: modal + *have* + V3. Never *has* or *had* after a modal.
- **Deduction:** *must* (sure yes) · *may / might / could* (perhaps) · *can't* (sure no). Past: *must have / might have / can't have* + V3.
- *should have* + V3 = right action, not done. *needn't have* + V3 = done, but not necessary. *could have* + V3 = possible, not done.
- *must* = the speaker's need; *have to* = a need from outside.
- *mustn't* = don't do it. *needn't / don't have to* = not necessary.
- *used to* + V1 (past habit) ≠ *be used to* + V4 (accustomed).
:::
