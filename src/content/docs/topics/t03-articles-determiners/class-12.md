## What you will learn

- What determiners are, and the six groups: **articles, demonstratives, possessives, quantifiers, distributives, numbers**
- Which determiners go with countable nouns, uncountable nouns, singular and plural nouns
- The pairs that cause the most errors:
  - *few / a few / the few*
  - *little / a little / the little*
  - *each / every*
  - *either / neither*
  - *much / many*
- How to answer "Fill in with suitable determiners" and error-spotting questions

## Why this matters

Determiners are small words, but they change the meaning a lot. *He has **few** friends* is sad. *He has **a few** friends* is fine. One small *a* makes the difference.

In the exam you will fill in blanks with determiners and spot errors like *~~much students~~* or *~~every of them~~*. Determiners also matter for [subject–verb agreement](/topics/t05-subject-verb-agreement/class-12/): *each student **has***, *both students **have***.

## The idea in simple words

Look at the word *mangoes* with different words before it.

> ***the** mangoes · **these** mangoes · **my** mangoes · **some** mangoes · **many** mangoes · **a few** mangoes · **both** mangoes · **five** mangoes*

The noun is the same. The word before it tells us **which** mangoes, **whose** mangoes, or **how many** mangoes. These words are called **determiners**. They "determine" (decide) what the noun refers to.

A determiner always comes at the **start** of the noun group, before any adjective:

> ***my** new phone* (not *~~new my phone~~*)
> ***these** sweet mangoes*

## Grammar words you need

**Determiner:** a word that comes before a noun and tells which one, whose, or how much / how many.

**Countable noun:** can be counted; has a plural: *book → books, student → students*.

**Uncountable noun:** cannot be counted; no plural: *water, rice, money, advice, information, furniture, luggage, news*.

**Quantifier:** a determiner that shows amount: *some, many, much, few, little, enough, all*.

**Distributive:** a determiner that refers to members of a group one by one: *each, every, either, neither*.

**Demonstrative:** a determiner that points: *this, that, these, those*.

**Cardinal number:** a counting number: *one, two, three*. **Ordinal number:** a number that shows order: *first, second, third*.

## The rules

### Rule 1: There are six groups of determiners.

| Group | Determiners | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Articles | a, an, the | ***the** bus* |
| Demonstratives | this, that, these, those | ***those** shoes* |
| Possessives | my, your, his, her, its, our, their | ***our** school* |
| Quantifiers | some, any, no, much, many, few, a few, little, a little, enough, several, all, both, half, a lot of | ***several** times* |
| Distributives | each, every, either, neither | ***each** student* |
| Numbers | one, two, three... / first, second, third... | ***three** books*, *the **first** prize* |

### Rule 2: Use *this* and *these* for things near you, and *that* and *those* for things far away. Match the number.

| | Near | Far |
|---|---|---|
| Singular | ***this** pen* | ***that** pen* |
| Plural | ***these** pens* | ***those** pens* |

> ***This** book is mine. **Those** books on the top shelf are Kavya's.*
> *~~These kind of questions~~* ✘ → ***This kind** of question* ✔ / ***These kinds** of questions* ✔

*That* and *those* also refer to the past: *In **those** days, there were no mobile phones.*

### Rule 3: Possessive determiners show who owns something. Never use an article with them.

> ***my** book, **your** bag, **his** cycle, **her** saree, **its** tail, **our** house, **their** car*

> *~~the my book~~* ✘ / *~~a my friend~~* ✘ → ***a friend of mine*** ✔

***Its*** (no apostrophe) is the possessive: *The dog wagged **its** tail.* ***It's*** = *it is*: ***It's** raining.*

Possessive determiners always need a noun after them. Without a noun, use the possessive **pronoun**: *This is **my** pen. This pen is **mine**.*

### Rule 4: Use *some* in positive sentences and *any* in negatives and questions.

> *I have **some** money.*
> *I don't have **any** money.*
> *Do you have **any** money?*

*Some* and *any* go with **plural countable** and **uncountable** nouns: *some books, some water, any questions, any milk*.

Two special uses:

- ***Some* in questions** when we offer something or expect "yes": *Would you like **some** tea?* / *Can I have **some** water?*
- ***Any* in positive sentences** to mean "it does not matter which": *You can take **any** book you like.*

***No*** = *not any*: *There is **no** milk.* = *There is**n't any** milk.*

### Rule 5: Use *many* with countable nouns and *much* with uncountable nouns.

| *many* + plural countable | *much* + uncountable |
|---|---|
| *many students, many books, many times* | *much water, much time, much money, much advice* |
| *How **many** brothers do you have?* | *How **much** sugar do you want?* |

*Much* is used mainly in **negatives and questions**. In positive sentences, we usually say *a lot of* or *lots of* (for both kinds):

> *I don't have **much** time.* ✔
> *I have **a lot of** time.* ✔ (*I have much time* sounds formal or odd.)

*Much* in positive sentences is fine with *so, too, as*: *There is **too much** noise.*

### Rule 6: *A few* means "some". *Few* means "almost none". *The few* means "all of the small number".

All three go with **plural countable** nouns.

| Form | Meaning | Feeling | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***a few*** | some, a small number | positive | *I have **a few** friends in Salem.* (I have some. That's nice.) |
| ***few*** | hardly any, almost none | negative | *He has **few** friends.* (almost no friends. That's sad.) |
| ***the few*** | all of the small number (that exist) | specific | *I gave him **the few** books I had.* (I had only a small number, and I gave all of them.) |

> ***A few** students passed.* (some passed)
> ***Few** students passed.* (almost all failed)

***Quite a few*** means "a fairly large number": *Quite a few people came.*

### Rule 7: *A little* means "some". *Little* means "almost none". *The little* means "all of the small amount".

The same pattern, but with **uncountable** nouns.

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***a little*** | some, a small amount | *There is **a little** milk in the jug.* (enough for a cup of tea) |
| ***little*** | hardly any | *There is **little** hope of finding him.* (almost no hope) |
| ***the little*** | all of the small amount | *She spent **the little** money she had on books.* |

> *Few* / *a few* → countable (*students, books*)
> *Little* / *a little* → uncountable (*milk, time, hope*)

### Rule 8: Use *enough* before a noun and after an adjective or adverb.

> *We have **enough** chairs.* (before a noun)
> *Is there **enough** rice for everyone?*
> *He is old **enough** to vote.* (after an adjective)
> *She didn't run fast **enough**.* (after an adverb)

*~~enough old~~* ✘ → *old **enough*** ✔

### Rule 9: Use *several* for "more than two, but not many", with plural countable nouns.

> *I have told you **several** times.*
> ***Several** students were absent today.*

### Rule 10: *All, both* and *half* can come before *the, my, these*. *Both* is used for two things only.

> ***All** students must attend.* / ***All the** students in my class passed.*
> ***Both** my brothers live in Chennai.* (I have two brothers.)
> ***Half the** class was absent.* / *I spent **half my** salary.*

*Of* is optional before a noun: *all (of) the students, both (of) my brothers, half (of) the cake*. With a **pronoun**, *of* is needed: ***all of** them, **both of** us, **half of** it*.

*Both* is only for **two**. For more than two, use *all*: *~~both the three boys~~* ✘ → ***all three** boys* ✔

### Rule 11: *Each* sees members one by one. *Every* sees them as a whole group. Both take a singular noun and a singular verb.

> ***Each** student **has** a different question paper.* (one by one: they are different)
> ***Every** student **has** passed.* (all of them, as a group)

| | *each* | *every* |
|---|---|---|
| Number | two or more | three or more (not used for two) |
| Focus | individual members, one by one | the whole group, all together |
| Alone as a pronoun? | Yes: ***Each** got a prize.* / ***Each of** the boys got a prize.* | No: *~~every of the boys~~* → ***every one of** the boys* |
| With *almost / nearly* | ✘ | ✔ *almost **every** house* |
| Repeated time | — | ***every** day, **every** two weeks* |

> *She wore a ring on **each** hand.* (two hands → *each*, not *every*)

*Each of* + plural noun + **singular** verb: ***Each of** the boys **has** a bat.*

### Rule 12: *Either* means "one or the other of two". *Neither* means "not one and not the other". Both take a singular noun.

> *You can take **either** road. Both go to Trichy.* (one or the other: it doesn't matter)
> ***Neither** answer is correct.* (not this one and not that one)
> *There are shops on **either** side of the road.* (= on both sides)

*Either of / neither of* + plural noun: ***Neither of** the answers **is** correct.* (A singular verb is correct in formal English. A plural verb is common in speech.)

*Either* and *neither* are only for **two**. For more than two, use *any* and *none*: ***None** of the five answers is correct.*

*Neither* is already negative. Do not use *not* with it: *~~Neither answer is not correct~~* ✘

### Rule 13: Ordinal numbers come before cardinal numbers. Numbers come after *the, my, these*.

> ***the first two** chapters* (not *~~the two first chapters~~*)
> ***my three** sisters*
> ***these five** books*
> ***the last three** days*

Ordinal numbers usually take *the*: ***the** second floor, **the** tenth class*.

### Rule 14: Determiners come in a fixed order.

| Before (pre-) | Central | After (post-) | Adjective | Noun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all, both, half | the, this, my... | numbers, many, few, several | | |
| ***all*** | ***the*** | | *new* | *students* |
| ***both*** | ***my*** | | *elder* | *brothers* |
| | ***the*** | ***first three*** | | *questions* |
| | ***her*** | ***many*** | *old* | *friends* |

Only **one** central determiner is used at a time: *~~the my book~~*, *~~this the book~~* ✘.

## Special cases

### 1. *Fewer* and *less*

*Fewer* + plural countable nouns. *Less* + uncountable nouns.

> ***fewer** students, **fewer** mistakes* / ***less** water, **less** time*

In informal English, *less* is often used with countable nouns (*less people*). In exams, use *fewer*.

### 2. *A lot of, lots of, plenty of*

These go with **both** countable and uncountable nouns: *a lot of books, a lot of rice*.

*A great deal of* + uncountable: *a great deal of money*. *A large number of* + countable: *a large number of people*.

### 3. *Many a* + singular noun + singular verb

> ***Many a** student **has** failed this paper.* (= many students have failed)

This is formal and literary, but it appears in exams.

### 4. *No* and *none*

*No* is a determiner (before a noun). *None* is a pronoun (alone).

> *There is **no** sugar.* / *There is **none** left.*

### 5. *Every* with a number

> *The bus comes **every** ten minutes.* / *He visits us **every** two weeks.*

Here *every* is followed by a plural noun, because of the number.

### 6. *Each other* and *one another*

These are not determiners, but they come up with *each*. Traditionally, *each other* is used for two, *one another* for more than two. In modern English they are used the same way.

> *Ravi and Meena help **each other**.*

### 7. *The few* and *the little* vs *few* and *little*

*The few* and *the little* are **not** negative. They mean "all of the small number / amount". The word *the* points to a specific group.

> ***The few** friends he has are very loyal.* (He has a small number of friends, and all of them are loyal.)

## Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *There are ~~much~~ students in the hall.* | *There are **many** students in the hall.* | *Students* is countable → *many*. |
| *I don't have ~~many~~ money.* | *I don't have **much** money.* | *Money* is uncountable → *much*. |
| *~~Every of~~ the boys got a prize.* | ***Every one of** the boys* / ***Each of** the boys got a prize.* | *Every* cannot stand alone before *of*. |
| *Each student ~~have~~ a book.* | *Each student **has** a book.* | *Each* takes a singular verb. |
| *She had a bangle on ~~every~~ wrist.* | *She had a bangle on **each** wrist.* | Two things → *each*, not *every*. |
| *~~Both~~ the three girls sang.* | ***All three** girls sang.* | *Both* is only for two. |
| *Neither of the answers ~~are not~~ correct.* | *Neither of the answers **is** correct.* | *Neither* is already negative, and takes a singular verb in formal English. |
| *Give me ~~few~~ minutes, please.* | *Give me **a few** minutes, please.* | The speaker means "some minutes", so use *a few*. |
| *There is ~~a few~~ milk in the jug.* | *There is **a little** milk in the jug.* | *Milk* is uncountable → *little*, not *few*. |
| *~~The two first~~ chapters are easy.* | ***The first two** chapters are easy.* | Ordinal number before cardinal number. |
| *There were ~~less~~ people this year.* | *There were **fewer** people this year.* | *People* is countable → *fewer* (in formal English). |
| *The dog hurt ~~it's~~ leg.* | *The dog hurt **its** leg.* | *Its* is the possessive. *It's* = *it is*. |

## How it comes in the exam

Common exercise types:

- **Fill in with suitable determiners** (sometimes from a box).
- **Choose the correct determiner**: *few / a few, much / many, each / every*.
- **Spot the error**.

### Steps for "Fill in with a suitable determiner"

1. **Look at the noun**: countable or uncountable? singular or plural?
2. **Look at the sentence type**: positive, negative or question? (*some* vs *any*, *much* in negatives)
3. **Find the meaning**: which one? whose? how many? one by one? two or more?
4. **Check the feeling**: positive (*a few, a little*) or negative (*few, little*)?
5. **Check the verb**: *each, every, either, neither* → singular verb.

| Noun type | Determiners that fit |
|---|---|
| Singular countable | a, an, the, this, that, each, every, either, neither, my... |
| Plural countable | the, these, those, some, any, many, few, a few, several, both, all, numbers |
| Uncountable | the, this, that, some, any, much, little, a little, all, enough |

### Worked example 1

> *There is ______ hope that he will recover. The doctors are worried.* (little / a little)

1. Noun: *hope* → uncountable → *little* or *a little*.
2. Feeling: *The doctors are worried* → negative.
3. Negative → *little* (= almost no hope).

**Answer:** *There is **little** hope that he will recover.*

### Worked example 2

> *______ of my two sisters is married. Both are still studying.* (Either / Neither)

1. Two sisters → *either* or *neither*.
2. *Both are still studying* → not one and not the other is married → negative.

**Answer:** ***Neither** of my two sisters is married.*

### Worked example 3

> *______ student in the class was given a certificate.* (Every / All)

1. Noun: *student* → singular countable.
2. *All* needs a plural noun (*all students*). *Every* takes a singular noun.

**Answer:** ***Every** student in the class was given a certificate.* (*Each* is also correct.)

### Worked example 4

> *Spot the error: I have very less friends in this city.*

1. Noun: *friends* → plural countable.
2. *Less* goes with uncountable nouns. For countable nouns, use *few*.
3. *Very few* = almost none (negative), which fits the meaning.

**Answer:** *I have very **few** friends in this city.*

### Worked example 5

> *Would you like ______ coffee?* (some / any)

1. Noun: *coffee* → uncountable.
2. Sentence type: a question, but it is an **offer**.
3. In offers, use *some*.

**Answer:** *Would you like **some** coffee?*

## Quick check

1. *I have ______ friends here, so I am not lonely.* (few / a few)
2. *How ______ rice do you need?* (much / many)
3. *There are trees on ______ side of the road.* (either / every)
4. *She gave away ______ money she had saved.* (little / the little)
5. Correct the error: *Each of the players have a new kit.*

<details>
<summary>Show answers</summary>

1. ***a few.*** *I am not lonely* is positive, so the speaker has some friends → *a few*.
2. ***much.*** *Rice* is uncountable → *much*.
3. ***either.*** A road has two sides. *Either side* means "both sides". *Every* is not used for two.
4. ***the little.*** She had a small amount, and she gave away all of it → *the little*.
5. ***Each of the players has a new kit.*** *Each* takes a singular verb, even with *of* + plural noun.

</details>

## Summary

:::tip[Remember]
- Six groups: articles · demonstratives · possessives · quantifiers · distributives · numbers.
- *many / few / a few / fewer* + **countable**. *much / little / a little / less* + **uncountable**.
- *a few, a little* = some (positive). *few, little* = almost none (negative). *the few, the little* = all of the small amount.
- *some* in positives and offers; *any* in negatives and questions.
- *each* = one by one, two or more, can stand alone. *every* = the whole group, three or more, needs a noun.
- *either / neither / both* = only two. All take a singular noun except *both*.
- *the first two* (ordinal before cardinal). One central determiner only: never *~~the my~~*.
:::
